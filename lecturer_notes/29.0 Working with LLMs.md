## Part 1: Introduction to Transformers

### 1.1 Background on NLP and Tasks

Natural Language Processing encompasses a wide range of tasks that require machines to understand, interpret, and generate human language. Before the transformer revolution, the field was dominated by recurrent architectures and complex pipelines.

**Core NLP Tasks:**

**Classification Tasks:** Sentiment analysis requires determining the emotional tone of text, such as identifying whether a product review expresses satisfaction or disappointment. Topic classification involves assigning documents to predefined categories, while spam detection distinguishes legitimate messages from unwanted content.

**Sequence Tasks:** Named Entity Recognition identifies and classifies entities like people, organizations, and locations within text. Part-of-speech tagging assigns grammatical categories to each word in a sentence. Machine translation converts text from one language to another while preserving meaning and nuance.

**Generation Tasks:** Text summarization condenses long documents into shorter versions that capture key information. Question answering systems provide specific answers to natural language queries. Dialog systems engage in multi-turn conversations with users.

The evolution from rule-based systems to statistical methods, then to neural approaches, and finally to transformer-based models represents a fundamental shift in how we approach these problems. Each advancement brought improvements in handling context, capturing semantic relationships, and generalizing across tasks.

**Key Papers:**
- "A Neural Probabilistic Language Model" (Bengio et al., 2003)
- "Natural Language Processing (Almost) from Scratch" (Collobert et al., 2011)

### 1.2 Tokenization

Tokenization converts raw text into discrete units that models can process. The choice of tokenization strategy fundamentally affects model performance, vocabulary size, and the ability to handle rare words or out-of-vocabulary terms.

**Tokenization Approaches:**

**Word-Level Tokenization:** This approach splits text on whitespace and punctuation, treating each word as a separate token. While intuitive, it struggles with morphological variations. For example, the words "run," "running," and "ran" would be three entirely separate tokens with no shared representation, despite their semantic relationship. Additionally, rare words create massive vocabularies, and unseen words cannot be represented at all.

**Character-Level Tokenization:** By treating individual characters as tokens, this method eliminates vocabulary issues entirely. Any text can be represented using a small set of characters. However, sequences become much longer, and the model must learn to compose characters into meaningful units. The phrase "transformer" becomes eleven separate tokens instead of one.

**Subword Tokenization:** Modern approaches like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), WordPiece, and Unigram strike a balance between word-level and character-level methods. These algorithms learn to merge frequently occurring character sequences into subwords. Common words remain intact, while rare words decompose into meaningful pieces.

**Example - BPE in Action:**

Consider the word "unhappiness" with initial vocabulary containing only characters. BPE might learn the following merges based on frequency in the training corpus:

```
Step 1: "un" appears frequently → merge to "un"
Step 2: "happy" appears frequently → merge to "happy"  
Step 3: "ness" appears frequently → merge to "ness"
Final tokenization: ["un", "happy", "ness"]
```

This allows the model to represent "unhappiness" compositionally while also being able to tokenize "happiness" as ["happy", "ness"] using shared subwords. The vocabulary size typically ranges from 30,000 to 50,000 tokens, balancing coverage and efficiency.

**SentencePiece Implementation:**

SentencePiece treats the input as a raw stream of Unicode characters, enabling language-agnostic tokenization. This proves particularly valuable for languages without clear word boundaries, such as Chinese or Japanese, or when dealing with multilingual data.

**Key Papers:**
- "Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units" (Sennrich et al., 2016)
- "SentencePiece: A simple and language independent approach" (Kudo & Richardson, 2018)

### 1.3 Embeddings

Embeddings map discrete tokens into continuous vector spaces where semantic relationships manifest as geometric patterns. This transformation enables neural networks to process linguistic information through algebraic operations.

**Vector Space Semantics:**

In a well-trained embedding space, semantically similar words cluster together. The word "king" might have a vector representation closer to "queen" and "monarch" than to "banana" or "telephone." These representations capture not just similarity but also relationships.

Consider the famous example: `vector("king") - vector("man") + vector("woman") ≈ vector("queen")`. This arithmetic suggests that embeddings encode gender relationships, royal status, and other semantic properties in different dimensions of the vector space.

**Embedding Dimensions:**

Early approaches used dimensions ranging from 50 to 300. Modern transformer models employ much larger embeddings, typically 768 for BERT-base, 1024 for BERT-large, or even 4096+ for large language models. Higher dimensions allow for richer representations but increase computational requirements and risk overfitting on smaller datasets.

**Key Papers:**
- "Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space" (Mikolov et al., 2013)
- "GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation" (Pennington et al., 2014)

### 1.4 Word2Vec

Word2Vec introduced an efficient approach to learning word embeddings from large corpora by predicting contextual relationships. The architecture comes in two flavors, each with distinct training objectives.

**Skip-Gram Architecture:**

Given a target word, Skip-Gram predicts the surrounding context words. For the sentence "The cat sat on the mat," when processing the target word "sat," the model attempts to predict "cat," "on," and potentially "The" and "the" depending on the window size.

The training objective maximizes the probability of context words given the target. Mathematically, for a target word w_t and context words within a window:

```
maximize: Σ log P(w_{t+j} | w_t)
```

This formulation encourages words appearing in similar contexts to have similar embeddings. The context window size (typically 5-10 words) controls how much surrounding information influences the representation.

**Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW):**

CBOW reverses the prediction direction, using context words to predict the target. For the same sentence, it would use "cat" and "on" (and possibly others) to predict "sat." This approach proves faster to train and works better for smaller datasets, though Skip-Gram generally produces better representations for rare words.

**Negative Sampling:**

Training on all possible word pairs becomes computationally prohibitive for large vocabularies. Negative sampling addresses this by treating the problem as binary classification: distinguish actual context pairs from randomly sampled negative pairs.

For each true pair (target, context), the model samples k noise words (typically 5-20) from the vocabulary according to their frequency. The objective becomes maximizing the probability of the true pair while minimizing probabilities of negative pairs:

```
log σ(v'_context · v_target) + Σ[k] log σ(-v'_noise · v_target)
```

**Key Papers:**
- "Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space" (Mikolov et al., 2013)
- "Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases" (Mikolov et al., 2013)

### 1.5 Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN)

RNNs process sequential data by maintaining a hidden state that updates as the model processes each element. This architecture naturally handles variable-length inputs and can theoretically capture long-range dependencies.

**Architecture and Information Flow:**

At each time step t, an RNN receives input x_t and the previous hidden state h_{t-1}, producing a new hidden state h_t:

```
h_t = tanh(W_hh · h_{t-1} + W_xh · x_t + b_h)
```

This recurrent connection allows information to persist across time steps. For language modeling, the hidden state accumulates information about all previously seen words, theoretically enabling the model to use this context when predicting the next word.

**Vanishing and Exploding Gradients:**

During backpropagation through time, gradients must flow backward through many time steps. The repeated multiplication of weight matrices causes gradients to either vanish (approach zero) or explode (grow unbounded).

Consider a gradient flowing back T steps. If the dominant eigenvalue of the weight matrix is less than one, the gradient shrinks exponentially as (λ)^T. If greater than one, it grows exponentially. This makes training difficult and limits the model's ability to learn long-range dependencies.

**Key Papers:**
- "Learning representations by back-propagating errors" (Rumelhart et al., 1986)
- "On the difficulty of training recurrent neural networks" (Pascanu et al., 2013)

### 1.6 Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)

LSTMs address the vanishing gradient problem through a sophisticated gating mechanism that controls information flow. The architecture maintains both a hidden state and a cell state, with gates regulating what information to keep, forget, or update.

**LSTM Components:**

**Forget Gate:** This gate determines what information to discard from the cell state. It looks at the current input and previous hidden state, outputting a value between 0 and 1 for each element in the cell state:

```
f_t = σ(W_f · [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f)
```

A value of 1 means "completely keep this," while 0 means "completely forget this." For example, when processing a sentence and encountering a new subject, the forget gate might discard information about the previous subject.

**Input Gate:** This determines what new information to store in the cell state. It consists of two parts: a sigmoid layer deciding which values to update, and a tanh layer creating a vector of candidate values:

```
i_t = σ(W_i · [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i)
C̃_t = tanh(W_C · [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_C)
```

**Cell State Update:** The old cell state C_{t-1} gets updated to C_t by forgetting selected information and adding new candidate values:

```
C_t = f_t * C_{t-1} + i_t * C̃_t
```

**Output Gate:** This determines what to output based on the cell state, filtering it through a sigmoid layer and tanh:

```
o_t = σ(W_o · [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o)
h_t = o_t * tanh(C_t)
```

**Why LSTMs Work:**

The cell state acts as a highway for information, allowing gradients to flow backward more easily. The additive nature of the cell state update (rather than multiplicative) helps prevent vanishing gradients. This enables LSTMs to capture dependencies spanning hundreds of time steps.

**Example Application:**

In sentiment analysis, an LSTM might need to remember the negation "not" appearing several words before an otherwise positive phrase. Processing "I did not think the movie was particularly good," the LSTM must maintain the negation through "think," "the," "movie," "was," and "particularly" before applying it to "good."

**Key Papers:**
- "Long Short-Term Memory" (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997)
- "LSTM: A Search Space Odyssey" (Greff et al., 2017)

### 1.7 Attention Mechanism

Attention revolutionized sequence modeling by allowing models to dynamically focus on relevant parts of the input rather than compressing everything into a fixed-size representation. This mechanism underpins the transformer architecture.

**Motivation and Intuition:**

Traditional encoder-decoder models compress the entire source sequence into a single context vector. For long sequences, this creates an information bottleneck. Attention addresses this by allowing the decoder to access all encoder hidden states, weighted by relevance.

Consider translating "The agreement on the European Economic Area was signed in August 1992" to French. When translating "August," the model should focus primarily on "August" and "1992," paying less attention to "agreement" or "European." Attention learns these relevance weights automatically.

**Attention Mechanism Mathematics:**

Given a query vector q and a set of key-value pairs (K, V), attention computes a weighted sum of values where weights come from the compatibility between the query and keys:

```
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(score(Q, K)) · V
```

The scoring function determines how well the query matches each key. Common choices include:

**Additive (Bahdanau) Attention:**
```
score(q, k_i) = v^T · tanh(W_1 · q + W_2 · k_i)
```

**Multiplicative (Luong) Attention:**
```
score(q, k_i) = q^T · k_i
```

**Scaled Dot-Product Attention:**
```
score(Q, K) = (Q · K^T) / sqrt(d_k)
```

The scaling factor prevents dot products from growing too large, which would push the softmax into regions with extremely small gradients.

**Example Calculation:**

Suppose we have a query q = [1, 0, 1] and keys K:
```
k_1 = [1, 0, 0]
k_2 = [0, 1, 1]  
k_3 = [1, 0, 1]
```

Computing dot products:
```
q · k_1 = 1
q · k_2 = 1
q · k_3 = 2
```

After softmax (ignoring scaling for simplicity):
```
α = [0.21, 0.21, 0.58]
```

The attention focuses most strongly on k_3, which has the highest compatibility with the query. If these keys represent words in a sentence, the model is attending primarily to the third word.

**Key Papers:**
- "Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate" (Bahdanau et al., 2015)
- "Effective Approaches to Attention-based Neural Machine Translation" (Luong et al., 2015)

### 1.8 Transformer Architecture

The Transformer eliminates recurrence entirely, relying solely on attention mechanisms to capture dependencies. This architectural choice enables massive parallelization and has become the foundation for modern NLP.

**Core Components:**

**Self-Attention:** Unlike encoder-decoder attention where queries come from the decoder and keys/values from the encoder, self-attention computes attention over the same sequence. Each position attends to all positions, learning which other positions are relevant.

**Multi-Head Attention:** Rather than performing a single attention function, multi-head attention runs multiple attention operations in parallel, each with different learned projections. This allows the model to attend to different aspects simultaneously.

```
MultiHead(Q, K, V) = Concat(head_1, ..., head_h) · W_O

where head_i = Attention(Q·W_Q^i, K·W_K^i, V·W_V^i)
```

With eight heads, one head might focus on syntactic relationships, another on semantic similarity, and another on positional proximity. The final concatenation combines these diverse perspectives.

**Position-wise Feed-Forward Networks:** After attention, each position passes through an identical feed-forward network consisting of two linear transformations with a ReLU activation:

```
FFN(x) = max(0, x·W_1 + b_1)·W_2 + b_2
```

This provides non-linear transformations and allows the model to process the attended information. The hidden dimension typically expands by a factor of four (e.g., 768 → 3072 → 768).

**Positional Encoding:** Since the transformer processes all positions simultaneously, it has no inherent notion of sequence order. Positional encodings add order information by injecting position-dependent patterns into the input embeddings.

The original formulation uses sinusoidal functions:
```
PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 10000^(2i/d))
PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 10000^(2i/d))
```

These functions have useful properties: they allow the model to extrapolate to longer sequences, and relative positions can be computed through linear transformations.

**Layer Normalization and Residual Connections:** Each sub-layer (attention or feed-forward) has a residual connection followed by layer normalization:

```
output = LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x))
```

Residual connections help gradients flow during training, while layer normalization stabilizes the learning process by normalizing activations across features.

**Complete Transformer Block:**

A single encoder block combines these elements:
```
1. Multi-head self-attention with residual and layer norm
2. Feed-forward network with residual and layer norm
```

The decoder adds a third component between these two:
```
1. Masked multi-head self-attention
2. Cross-attention to encoder outputs  
3. Feed-forward network
```

**Example - Attention Pattern:**

For the sentence "The cat sat on the mat," self-attention might learn patterns like:
- "cat" attends strongly to "The" (determiner-noun)
- "sat" attends to "cat" (subject-verb) and "mat" (verb-object)
- "on" attends to both "sat" and "mat" (preposition connecting verb and object)

These patterns emerge from training rather than being hard-coded, allowing the model to learn task-specific attention strategies.

**Key Papers:**
- "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017)

## Part 2: Transformer-Based Models and Optimizations

### 2.1 Attention Approximation

Standard self-attention has quadratic complexity O(n²) in sequence length, making it prohibitively expensive for long sequences. Various approximation methods reduce this complexity while maintaining most of attention's benefits.

**Sparse Attention Patterns:**

Rather than attending to all positions, sparse attention restricts each position to attend to a subset. Patterns include:

**Local Windows:** Each position attends only to nearby positions within a fixed window. For sequence length n and window size w, complexity becomes O(n·w). This works well when most relevant information is nearby, as in many language tasks.

**Strided Patterns:** Positions attend to every k-th position, capturing long-range dependencies with reduced computation. Combined with local windows, this creates patterns like those in Longformer.

**Random Patterns:** Randomly select positions to attend to, averaging over multiple random samples. This provides a statistical approximation of full attention.

**Linformer Approach:**

Linformer projects keys and values into lower-dimensional spaces before computing attention. If the original sequence has length n and we project to dimension k (with k << n), attention complexity reduces from O(n²·d) to O(n·k·d).

The key insight: the attention matrix often has low rank, meaning most information concentrates in a small number of dimensions. Projections capture this essential information while discarding redundant components.

**Performer and Kernel Methods:**

Performers reformulate attention as a kernel operation, enabling linear complexity O(n). Instead of computing attention weights explicitly through softmax, they use random feature approximations:

```
Traditional: softmax(Q·K^T)·V
Performer: φ(Q)·(φ(K)^T·V)
```

where φ represents random feature maps. The associativity of matrix multiplication allows computing φ(K)^T·V first (O(n) complexity) rather than Q·K^T (O(n²)).

**Key Papers:**
- "Generating Long Sequences with Sparse Transformers" (Child et al., 2019)
- "Longformer: The Long-Document Transformer" (Beltagy et al., 2020)
- "Linformer: Self-Attention with Linear Complexity" (Wang et al., 2020)
- "Rethinking Attention with Performers" (Choromanski et al., 2021)

### 2.2 Multi-Head, Multi-Query, and Grouped-Query Attention

Different attention variants trade off between model quality and computational efficiency, particularly important for inference speed and memory usage.

**Multi-Head Attention (MHA):**

Standard multi-head attention maintains separate key, value, and query projections for each head:

```
For h heads, each with dimension d_k = d_model / h:
head_i = Attention(Q·W_Q^i, K·W_K^i, V·W_V^i)
```

This requires h sets of parameters for keys and values. During autoregressive generation, all these key-value pairs must be cached, creating a memory bottleneck that grows with sequence length and batch size.

**Multi-Query Attention (MQA):**

MQA shares a single key and value projection across all query heads, dramatically reducing the key-value cache size:

```
For all heads: use same K·W_K and V·W_V
Only queries differ: Q·W_Q^i for each head i
```

This reduces memory by a factor of h but may hurt model quality since heads cannot learn specialized key-value representations. Despite this, MQA often achieves competitive performance, especially in decoder-only architectures.

**Grouped-Query Attention (GQA):**

GQA interpolates between MHA and MQA by sharing keys and values within groups of heads. For g groups of h/g heads each:

```
Group 1: heads 1 to h/g share K·W_K^1 and V·W_V^1
Group 2: heads h/g+1 to 2h/g share K·W_K^2 and V·W_V^2
...
```

This provides a tunable tradeoff: g=1 gives MQA (maximum efficiency), while g=h gives MHA (maximum expressivity). Empirically, using 2-8 groups often achieves near-MHA quality with significant speed improvements.

**Example Comparison:**

For a model with 32 attention heads and hidden dimension 4096:
- MHA: 32 separate key-value projections, cache size = 32 × seq_len × 128
- GQA (8 groups): 8 shared projections, cache size = 8 × seq_len × 128 (4× reduction)
- MQA: 1 shared projection, cache size = 1 × seq_len × 128 (32× reduction)

**Key Papers:**
- "Fast Transformer Decoding: One Write-Head is All You Need" (Shazeer, 2019)
- "GQA: Training Generalized Multi-Query Transformer Models" (Ainslie et al., 2023)

### 2.3 Position Embeddings

Position information proves crucial for transformers to understand sequence order. Different encoding schemes offer various tradeoffs in expressivity, computational efficiency, and generalization to longer sequences.

**Learned Absolute Positions:**

The simplest approach learns a unique embedding for each position index:

```
x_i = token_embedding(i) + position_embedding(i)
```

These position embeddings are learned parameters optimized during training. While straightforward, this approach has limitations: the model cannot generalize to positions longer than those seen during training, and each position is treated independently without inherent notions of distance or relative ordering.

**Sinusoidal Positions:**

The original Transformer uses fixed sinusoidal functions that don't require learning:

```
PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 10000^(2i/d))
PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 10000^(2i/d))
```

The varying frequencies across dimensions create unique patterns for each position. A valuable property: for any fixed offset k, PE(pos+k) can be represented as a linear function of PE(pos), allowing the model to learn relative positions.

**Relative Position Representations:**

Rather than encoding absolute positions, these methods directly model the relationship between positions. When computing attention from position i to position j, the model uses a learned embedding for the offset i-j.

This proves particularly useful for tasks where absolute position matters less than relative distance, such as identifying whether one word modifies another regardless of where in the sequence they appear.

**Key Papers:**
- "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017)
- "Self-Attention with Relative Position Representations" (Shaw et al., 2018)

### 2.4 Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE)

RoPE encodes position information by rotating token representations in a way that naturally captures relative positions through inner products. This elegant approach has become standard in modern large language models.

**Mathematical Foundation:**

RoPE applies a rotation matrix that depends on the absolute position, but crucially, the inner product between two rotated vectors depends only on their relative position.

For a vector x at position m, RoPE applies a rotation:

```
f(x, m) = R_Θ,m · x

where R_Θ,m is a rotation matrix depending on position m
```

The key property: when computing attention between positions m and n, the dot product becomes:

```
(R_Θ,m · q)^T · (R_Θ,n · k) = q^T · R_Θ,n-m · k
```

This depends only on the relative position n-m, not the absolute positions. The rotation effectively encodes relative position information into the attention computation itself.

**Implementation Details:**

In practice, RoPE divides the embedding dimension into pairs and applies 2D rotations. For dimension pair (2i, 2i+1) and position m:

```
[x_2i']     [cos(m·θ_i)  -sin(m·θ_i)]   [x_2i]
[x_2i+1'] = [sin(m·θ_i)   cos(m·θ_i)] · [x_2i+1]
```

Different pairs rotate at different frequencies determined by θ_i, creating unique patterns across the embedding space. The frequency decay (typically θ_i = 10000^(-2i/d)) ensures lower dimensions capture long-range dependencies while higher dimensions focus on local patterns.

**Advantages:**

RoPE offers several benefits over alternatives. It naturally extends to longer sequences without retraining since rotation operations work for any position. The model learns relative position relationships directly through the attention mechanism. No additional position embedding parameters are required, improving parameter efficiency. The formulation is computationally efficient, adding minimal overhead to attention computation.

**Applications in Modern Models:**

RoPE appears in numerous state-of-the-art models including GPT-NeoX, PaLM, LLaMA series, Mistral, and many others. Its effectiveness for long-context modeling makes it particularly valuable for applications requiring extended context windows.

**Key Papers:**
- "RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding" (Su et al., 2021)

### 2.5 Transformer-Based Architectures

Different architectural patterns serve different purposes, from understanding tasks to generation to hybrid approaches that combine both capabilities.

**Encoder-Only (BERT-style):**

These models process the entire input simultaneously using bidirectional attention. Each token can attend to all other tokens in both directions, building rich contextual representations. This architecture excels at understanding tasks like classification, named entity recognition, and extractive question answering.

The training objective typically involves masked language modeling: randomly mask tokens and predict them using bidirectional context. This forces the model to learn deep bidirectional representations.

**Decoder-Only (GPT-style):**

These models use causal (left-to-right) attention, where each token can only attend to previous tokens. This autoregressive structure naturally supports text generation by predicting the next token based on all previous tokens.

Training uses standard language modeling: predict each token given all previous tokens. The same architecture used for training can directly perform generation at inference time by sampling tokens autoregressively.

**Encoder-Decoder (T5-style):**

This architecture combines both components: an encoder processes the input bidirectionally, and a decoder generates the output autoregressively while attending to encoder outputs through cross-attention.

This proves particularly effective for sequence-to-sequence tasks like translation, summarization, and instruction following, where input and output have different structures or lengths.

**Architecture Comparison:**

Consider the task of answering "What is the capital of France?" given context about European geography:

- Encoder-only: Processes question and context bidirectionally, classifies or extracts the answer span ("Paris") 
- Decoder-only: Generates the answer autoregressively, treating the question and context as a prompt
- Encoder-decoder: Encodes the question and context, then generates the answer while attending to the encoding

**Key Papers:**
- "BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers" (Devlin et al., 2019)
- "Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners" (GPT-2, Radford et al., 2019)
- "Exploring the Limits of Transfer Learning with a Unified T5" (Raffel et al., 2020)

### 2.6 BERT and Derivatives

BERT introduced pre-training on large unlabeled corpora followed by fine-tuning on specific tasks, a paradigm that revolutionized NLP. Numerous derivatives extended and improved upon the original architecture.

**BERT Architecture and Training:**

BERT uses a bidirectional encoder with two pre-training objectives:

**Masked Language Modeling (MLM):** Randomly mask fifteen percent of tokens and predict them using bidirectional context. For the input "The cat sat on the [MASK]", the model must predict "mat" using context from both directions. Eighty percent of chosen tokens are replaced with [MASK], ten percent with random tokens, and ten percent left unchanged to reduce the mismatch between pre-training and fine-tuning.

**Next Sentence Prediction (NSP):** Given two sentences A and B, predict whether B actually follows A in the original text. This helps the model learn relationships between sentences, useful for tasks like question answering and natural language inference.

The model comes in two sizes: BERT-base (12 layers, 768 hidden dimensions, 12 attention heads, 110M parameters) and BERT-large (24 layers, 1024 hidden dimensions, 16 attention heads, 340M parameters).

**RoBERTa - Robustly Optimized BERT:**

RoBERTa demonstrated that BERT was significantly undertrained. Key improvements include:

- Training longer with larger batches (125M tokens vs. 3.3B tokens)
- Removing next sentence prediction (found to hurt performance)
- Training on longer sequences (512 tokens throughout rather than ramping up)
- Dynamically changing masking patterns each epoch rather than static masking
- Using byte-level BPE instead of character-level

These changes yielded substantial improvements on downstream tasks without changing the architecture.

**ALBERT - A Lite BERT:**

ALBERT reduces parameters through two techniques:

**Factorized Embedding Parameterization:** Rather than having the embedding dimension equal the hidden dimension, ALBERT projects smaller embeddings into the hidden space:
```
Embedding (E=128) → Projection → Hidden (H=768)
```

This reduces parameters since vocabulary size V multiplies by the smaller E rather than H.

**Cross-Layer Parameter Sharing:** All layers share the same parameters. Instead of learning separate transformations for each of the 12 or 24 layers, one set of parameters is used throughout. This dramatically reduces parameters while maintaining depth.

Additionally, ALBERT replaces NSP with sentence-order prediction, a more challenging task that improves downstream performance.

**DeBERTa - Decoding-Enhanced BERT:**

DeBERTa introduces disentangled attention, representing each token with two vectors: one for content and one for position. Attention weights are computed using:
```
- Content-to-content
- Content-to-position  
- Position-to-content
```

This allows the model to better capture dependencies between content and relative positions. DeBERTa also uses an enhanced mask decoder that incorporates absolute positions during fine-tuning, improving performance on tasks requiring position awareness.

**Key Papers:**
- "BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers" (Devlin et al., 2019)
- "RoBERTa: A Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach" (Liu et al., 2019)
- "ALBERT: A Lite BERT for Self-supervised Learning" (Lan et al., 2020)
- "DeBERTa: Decoding-enhanced BERT with Disentangled Attention" (He et al., 2021)

## Part 3: Large Language Models

### 3.1 Definition and Architecture

Large Language Models are transformer-based neural networks trained on massive text corpora, typically containing hundreds of billions to trillions of tokens. Their scale enables emergent capabilities not present in smaller models.

**Scaling Dimensions:**

LLMs scale across three dimensions: model size (measured in parameters, ranging from billions to hundreds of billions), training data (from hundreds of billions to over a trillion tokens), and compute (measured in FLOPs or training time).

Research has established scaling laws describing how model performance improves with each dimension. These laws suggest that performance follows predictable power-law relationships with scale, enabling forecasting of larger models' capabilities.

**Emergent Abilities:**

As models scale, they develop capabilities not explicitly trained for. These include few-shot learning (performing tasks from just a few examples without gradient updates), chain-of-thought reasoning (breaking complex problems into steps), and task composition (combining multiple skills to solve novel problems).

For example, a large enough model might learn to translate between languages it has never seen paired during training by composing its knowledge of each language separately.

**Common Architectures:**

Modern LLMs predominantly use decoder-only transformers (GPT-style). This choice stems from several advantages: simpler architecture than encoder-decoder models, natural fit for autoregressive generation, and efficient scaling to very large sizes.

Key architectural decisions include using RoPE or ALiBi position encodings for long context support, employing grouped-query attention for efficient inference, applying RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm for better performance, and using SwiGLU or GeGLU activations in feed-forward networks instead of ReLU.

**Key Papers:**
- "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" (GPT-3, Brown et al., 2020)
- "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (Kaplan et al., 2020)
- "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (Hoffmann et al., 2022)

### 3.2 Mixture of Experts

Mixture of Experts (MoE) architectures increase model capacity while keeping compute costs manageable by routing different inputs to different expert networks, with only a subset activated for any given input.

**Core Concept:**

Instead of every input passing through the same feed-forward network, MoE replaces these networks with multiple expert modules. A gating network decides which experts to use for each input:

```
MoE(x) = Σ G(x)_i · Expert_i(x)
```

where G(x) is the gating function producing weights for each expert.

**Routing Strategies:**

**Top-K Routing:** The gating network selects the top K experts with highest weights for each token, setting other weights to zero. Typically K=1 or K=2. This ensures bounded computation per token.

**Expert Capacity:** To balance load across experts, each expert has a maximum capacity (number of tokens it can process). If an expert's capacity is exceeded, overflow tokens either go to the next highest-scored expert or bypass the MoE layer entirely.

**Load Balancing:** Without intervention, routing tends to concentrate on a few experts, wasting capacity and reducing model expressivity. Load balancing losses encourage uniform expert usage:

```
L_aux = α · Σ (fraction of tokens to expert_i) · (expert_i's average score)
```

This auxiliary loss penalizes scenarios where popular experts receive high scores, encouraging the router to distribute load.

**Example - Switch Transformer:**

Switch Transformer uses a simplified MoE with top-1 routing (each token goes to exactly one expert). For a model with 128 experts and top-1 routing:

- Input token computes gating scores for all 128 experts
- Token routes to the single expert with highest score  
- Only that expert's parameters activate
- Total FLOPs per token ≈ FLOPs of a single expert

This achieves better performance than a dense model of the same FLOP budget by dramatically increasing total parameters while keeping active parameters constant.

**Advantages and Challenges:**

MoE models offer several benefits: they can scale to trillions of parameters while maintaining reasonable compute costs, enable specialization where different experts learn different skills or domains, and often train faster than dense models at the same performance level.

However, they also present challenges: inference requires loading all experts into memory even though only a few activate, communication overhead exists when experts are distributed across devices, training instability can occur from routing dynamics, and potential mode collapse happens where few experts do most of the work.

**Key Papers:**
- "Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated MoE Layer" (Shazeer et al., 2017)
- "Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models" (Fedus et al., 2021)
- "Mixtral of Experts" (Jiang et al., 2024)

### 3.3 Context Length and Temperature

These hyperparameters fundamentally shape how language models process information and generate text.

**Context Length:**

The context window determines how many previous tokens the model can attend to when predicting the next token. Early transformers used 512 or 1024 tokens, while modern LLMs range from 4K to 128K+ tokens.

**Context Length Implications:**

Longer contexts enable processing entire documents or books in a single forward pass, maintaining conversation history over extended dialogues, and providing more comprehensive information for complex reasoning tasks. However, they also increase computational costs quadratically with standard attention and risk diluting attention across too many tokens for focused tasks.

**Extending Context Length:**

Several techniques enable models to handle longer contexts than their training length:

**Position Interpolation:** Scale position encodings to fit longer sequences into the training range. If trained on positions 0-2048, positions 0-4096 map linearly to 0-2048:
```
position_new = position_original · (training_length / inference_length)
```

**YaRN (Yet another RoPE extensioN):** Improves upon basic interpolation by applying different scaling to different frequency components of RoPE, preserving local information while extending global context.

**ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases):** Instead of positional encodings, adds linear biases to attention scores based on distance. This naturally extends to any sequence length without modification.

**Temperature:**

Temperature τ controls the randomness of sampling from the model's output distribution. Before applying softmax to logits, divide by temperature:

```
P(token_i) = exp(logit_i / τ) / Σ exp(logit_j / τ)
```

**Temperature Effects:**

At temperature 1.0, the model uses its natural probability distribution. Lower temperatures (0.1-0.7) make the distribution sharper, increasing the probability of likely tokens and decreasing that of unlikely ones—resulting in more deterministic, focused outputs. Higher temperatures (1.0-2.0) flatten the distribution, giving unlikely tokens more probability—producing more creative, diverse, and potentially surprising outputs. At temperature approaching 0, sampling becomes equivalent to always choosing the most likely token (greedy decoding). At very high temperatures, sampling approaches uniform randomness.

**Example:**

Consider logits for three tokens: [2.0, 1.0, 0.5]

Temperature 0.5:
```
Probabilities: [0.73, 0.20, 0.07] (concentrated on top choice)
```

Temperature 1.0:  
```
Probabilities: [0.58, 0.24, 0.18] (moderate distribution)
```

Temperature 2.0:
```
Probabilities: [0.42, 0.31, 0.27] (more uniform)
```

**Key Papers:**
- "Train Short, Test Long: Attention with Linear Biases Enables Input Length Extrapolation" (Press et al., 2022)
- "Extending Context Window of Large Language Models via Position Interpolation" (Chen et al., 2023)
- "YaRN: Efficient Context Window Extension of LLMs" (Peng et al., 2023)

### 3.4 Sampling Strategies

Different sampling strategies balance between using the model's learned distribution and introducing controlled randomness to improve generation quality.

**Greedy Decoding:**

Always select the highest probability token at each step. This deterministic approach guarantees finding the most likely token sequence under a first-order approximation but often produces repetitive or generic text and can lead to degenerate repetition loops.

**Beam Search:**

Maintain B hypothesis sequences (beams) and expand the top B sequences at each step:

```
For beam width B=3 and vocabulary size V:
1. Start with one sequence
2. Generate V continuations
3. Keep top B sequences by cumulative probability
4. Repeat until reaching max length or all beams end
```

This explores multiple high-probability paths simultaneously but requires more computation (B times greedy decoding) and can still produce generic outputs due to likelihood maximization.

**Top-K Sampling:**

Restrict sampling to the K most likely tokens, redistributing their probabilities:

```
1. Compute probabilities for all tokens
2. Set all but top K to zero
3. Renormalize remaining K probabilities
4. Sample from this distribution
```

This prevents sampling from very unlikely tokens that might break coherence while introducing randomness. However, K is fixed regardless of the distribution shape; sometimes the top 10 tokens contain ninety percent of probability mass, other times they contain only thirty percent.

**Top-P (Nucleus) Sampling:**

Dynamically select the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds threshold P:

```
1. Sort tokens by probability
2. Find smallest set with cumulative probability ≥ P
3. Renormalize and sample from this set
```

With P=0.9, the model samples from however many tokens are needed to reach ninety percent cumulative probability. This adapts to the distribution's sharpness; focused distributions might sample from just 2-3 tokens, while uniform distributions might include dozens.

**Example Comparison:**

Consider a probability distribution: [0.5, 0.2, 0.15, 0.08, 0.03, 0.02, 0.01, ...]

- Greedy: Always chooses token 1 (0.5)
- Top-K (K=3): Samples from {0.59, 0.24, 0.17} (renormalized top 3)
- Top-P (P=0.9): Samples from {0.56, 0.22, 0.17, 0.09} (renormalized top 4, cumulative = 0.93)

**Typical Decoding:**

A variant of top-P that filters out tokens in the "typical set" based on information content rather than raw probability. Tokens with conditional entropy close to the expected entropy are preferred, filtering both very surprising (high information) and very unsurprising (low information) tokens.

**Practical Guidelines:**

For factual, focused tasks, use lower temperature (0.3-0.7) with top-K or top-P. For creative writing, use higher temperature (0.8-1.2) with top-P=0.9. For code generation, use temperature 0.2 with top-P=0.95. For chatbots, use temperature 0.7-0.9 with top-P=0.9.

**Key Papers:**
- "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration" (Holtzman et al., 2020)
- "Typical Decoding for Natural Language Generation" (Meister et al., 2022)

### 3.5 Prompting and In-Context Learning

In-context learning allows models to perform tasks by conditioning on examples provided in the prompt, without any parameter updates.

**Zero-Shot Prompting:**

Present the task description without examples:

```
Translate the following English text to French:
"The weather is beautiful today"
Translation:
```

The model must rely entirely on its pre-trained knowledge and understanding of the instruction. Performance depends heavily on how well the task was represented in pre-training data.

**Few-Shot Prompting:**

Provide examples of the desired input-output behavior:

```
Translate English to French:

English: Hello
French: Bonjour

English: Thank you
French: Merci

English: The weather is beautiful today
French:
```

Examples help the model understand the task format, desired output style, and edge cases. Generally, 1-5 examples suffice for many tasks, though more complex tasks benefit from additional examples.

**Prompt Engineering Principles:**

Effective prompts follow several principles. Be specific and detailed about the desired output format and constraints. Provide diverse examples covering different cases and potential edge cases. Use consistent formatting across examples to reduce ambiguity. Include reasoning or explanations in examples for complex tasks. Experiment with prompt ordering, as model performance can vary based on example sequence.

**Example - Sentiment Analysis:**

Poor prompt:
```
"The movie was okay" → 
```

Better prompt:
```
Classify the sentiment of the following review as Positive, Negative, or Neutral:

Review: "This film exceeded all my expectations! Amazing performances."
Sentiment: Positive

Review: "Terrible pacing and poor character development."
Sentiment: Negative

Review: "The movie was okay"
Sentiment:
```

The better prompt clearly specifies the task, output format, and provides diverse examples.

**Role Prompting:**

Assign the model a role or persona to guide its responses:

```
You are an expert data scientist explaining concepts to a beginner...
```

This helps set appropriate tone, vocabulary level, and perspective.

**Key Papers:**
- "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" (Brown et al., 2020)
- "Learning to Retrieve Prompts for In-Context Learning" (Rubin et al., 2022)

### 3.6 Chain of Thought

Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting elicits step-by-step reasoning from language models, dramatically improving performance on complex reasoning tasks.

**Basic Chain of Thought:**

Instead of asking for direct answers, prompt the model to show its reasoning:

```
Question: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis balls. 
Each can has 3 tennis balls. How many tennis balls does he have now?

Answer: Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 balls each is 6 balls. 
5 + 6 = 11. Roger has 11 tennis balls.
```

The explicit reasoning steps help the model break down complex problems, catch errors through intermediate verification, and apply learned reasoning patterns to new problems.

**Zero-Shot Chain of Thought:**

Simply adding "Let's think step by step" to prompts elicits reasoning without examples:

```
Q: If John has 3 apples and gives away 2, how many does he have?
A: Let's think step by step.
```

The model then generates intermediate reasoning before producing the final answer. This simple technique often matches or exceeds few-shot performance.

**Self-Consistency:**

Generate multiple reasoning paths with sampling, then select the most common final answer:

```
1. Sample k reasoning chains (e.g., k=40) using temperature > 0
2. Extract final answer from each chain
3. Return majority answer
```

This works because correct reasoning paths tend to converge on the same answer, while errors often lead to different incorrect answers. Self-consistency can significantly improve accuracy on math and reasoning tasks.

**Example - Math Problem:**

Question: A restaurant serves 120 customers on Monday, 20% more on Tuesday, and 15% fewer on Wednesday than Tuesday. How many customers on Wednesday?

Chain of Thought reasoning:
```
Step 1: Monday has 120 customers
Step 2: Tuesday has 20% more than Monday
  - 20% of 120 = 0.20 × 120 = 24
  - Tuesday = 120 + 24 = 144
Step 3: Wednesday has 15% fewer than Tuesday
  - 15% of 144 = 0.15 × 144 = 21.6
  - Wednesday = 144 - 21.6 = 122.4
  - Rounding to 122 customers
```

**Limitations:**

Chain of thought is most effective for problems with clear intermediate steps. It may not help with tasks requiring knowledge retrieval rather than reasoning. The quality depends on whether the model has learned valid reasoning patterns during pre-training. For very complex problems, even step-by-step reasoning can go astray.

**Key Papers:**
- "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models" (Wei et al., 2022)
- "Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners" (Kojima et al., 2022)
- "Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning" (Wang et al., 2023)

## Part 4: LLM Training

### 4.1 Pretraining

Pretraining creates the foundational model by learning from vast amounts of unlabeled text. This phase requires the most compute and determines the model's core capabilities.

**Training Objective:**

The standard objective is causal language modeling: predict the next token given all previous tokens:

```
L = -Σ log P(x_t | x_1, ..., x_{t-1})
```

This simple objective teaches the model grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and world knowledge implicitly present in the training data.

**Data Preparation:**

High-quality pretraining data is crucial. Modern approaches combine diverse sources:

**Common Crawl:** Web scrapes provide scale (trillions of tokens) but require heavy filtering to remove low-quality content, duplicate text, personally identifiable information, and toxic content.

**Books:** Provide long-form coherent text teaching narrative structure and deep reasoning.

**Code:** Teaches logical reasoning, structured thinking, and programming abilities.

**Scientific Papers:** Contribute technical knowledge and formal reasoning patterns.

**Wikipedia:** Offers factual information with citations and well-structured content.

Data preprocessing involves deduplication to remove repeated content, quality filtering based on language models or heuristics, personal information redaction, and balancing to prevent over-representation of common sources.

**Training Dynamics:**

**Learning Rate Scheduling:** Typically follows a warmup period (linear increase over initial training) followed by cosine decay or inverse square root decay. The peak learning rate depends on model size and batch size.

**Batch Size Scaling:** Larger batches enable better hardware utilization but require learning rate adjustments. The learning rate often scales with the square root of batch size.

**Gradient Clipping:** Prevents instability from occasional large gradients, typically clipping to a norm of 1.0.

**Example Training Run:**

For a 7B parameter model:
```
- Training data: 2 trillion tokens
- Batch size: 4 million tokens
- Training steps: 500,000
- Learning rate: 3e-4 (peak)
- Warmup: 2000 steps
- Training time: ~2-3 months on 256 GPUs
```

**Compute-Optimal Training:**

The Chinchilla paper established that most models are undertrained relative to their size. For a given compute budget, optimal performance comes from balancing model size and training tokens roughly equally. A 10x increase in compute should split into ~3.3x larger models trained on ~3.3x more data.

This finding led to smaller, better-trained models often outperforming larger undertrained models (e.g., 70B trained on 2T tokens vs 175B trained on 300B tokens).

**Key Papers:**
- "Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners" (Radford et al., 2019)
- "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (Hoffmann et al., 2022)
- "The RefinedWeb Dataset for Falcon LLM" (Penedo et al., 2023)

### 4.2 Quantization

Quantization reduces model precision from 32-bit or 16-bit floating point to 8-bit, 4-bit, or even lower representations, dramatically reducing memory and enabling deployment on resource-constrained devices.

**Quantization Basics:**

Standard training uses FP32 (32-bit floating point) or mixed precision with FP16. Quantization maps these high-precision values to lower-precision representations:

**Linear Quantization:**
```
quantized_value = round((fp_value - zero_point) / scale)
scale = (max_val - min_val) / (2^bits - 1)
```

**Symmetric Quantization:**
```
scale = max(|max_val|, |min_val|) / (2^(bits-1) - 1)
zero_point = 0
```

**Post-Training Quantization (PTQ):**

After training completes, quantize the model weights without retraining. This is fast and simple but can cause accuracy degradation, especially at aggressive compression levels.

**Calibration:** Run representative data through the model to collect activation statistics, determining optimal scaling factors for each layer.

**GPTQ (Gradient-Based PTQ):**

GPTQ minimizes quantization error using second-order information. For each layer, it solves:

```
argmin ||WX - ŴX||²
```

where W are original weights, Ŵ are quantized weights, and X represents typical activations. By using Hessian information, GPTQ achieves better accuracy than naive rounding, enabling 4-bit quantization with minimal degradation.

**GGUF and Variants:**

GGUF (formerly GGML) provides efficient quantization formats optimized for CPU inference:

- Q4_0: 4-bit weights with FP16 scales
- Q5_1: 5-bit weights with better accuracy
- Q8_0: 8-bit weights for higher quality

These formats balance model size, accuracy, and inference speed for deployment on consumer hardware.

**Quantization-Aware Training (QAT):**

Simulate quantization during training by inserting fake quantization operations:

```
1. Forward pass: Quantize weights/activations, then dequantize
2. Backward pass: Straight-through estimator for gradients
3. Update: Update full-precision weights
```

This allows the model to adapt to quantization constraints, typically achieving better accuracy than PTQ but requiring more effort.

**Mixed Precision Quantization:**

Different layers use different precision based on sensitivity analysis:
```
- Embedding layers: 8-bit (large but less sensitive)
- Attention layers: 8-bit or 6-bit
- Feed-forward layers: 4-bit (very large, moderately sensitive)
- Output layers: 8-bit (small but sensitive)
```

**Example - Memory Savings:**

For a 7B parameter model:
```
- FP32: 7B × 4 bytes = 28 GB
- FP16: 7B × 2 bytes = 14 GB
- INT8: 7B × 1 byte = 7 GB
- 4-bit: 7B × 0.5 bytes = 3.5 GB
```

4-bit quantization enables running 7B models on consumer GPUs with 8-12 GB VRAM.

**Key Papers:**
- "LLM.int8(): 8-bit Matrix Multiplication for Transformers" (Dettmers et al., 2022)
- "GPTQ: Accurate Post-Training Quantization for GPT" (Frantar et al., 2023)
- "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs" (Dettmers et al., 2023)

### 4.3 Hardware Optimization

Efficient training and inference require careful optimization of how models utilize GPU memory and compute resources.

**Memory Hierarchy:**

Modern GPUs have multiple memory levels with different characteristics:

**Registers:** Fastest access (1 cycle) but extremely limited capacity. Used for immediate operands.

**Shared Memory:** Fast access (~20 cycles), shared within a thread block. Critical for efficient matrix operations.

**L1/L2 Cache:** Automatic caching of frequently accessed data.

**Global Memory (HBM):** Large capacity (40-80 GB on modern GPUs) but slower access (~400 cycles).

**Kernel Fusion:**

Combine multiple operations into a single GPU kernel to reduce memory traffic:

```
Instead of:
  1. Load data → Compute LayerNorm → Store
  2. Load data → Compute Activation → Store
  3. Load data → Compute Dropout → Store

Fused kernel:
  Load data once → LayerNorm → Activation → Dropout → Store once
```

This reduces global memory accesses by 3x and often improves speed by 2-3x.

**FlashAttention:**

Standard attention implementation materializes the full N×N attention matrix in memory:

```
1. Compute Q·K^T (N×N matrix)
2. Apply softmax
3. Multiply by V
```

For sequences of 2048 tokens, this creates a 2048×2048 = 4M element matrix. FlashAttention recomputes attention on-the-fly in blocks that fit in shared memory:

```
1. Divide Q, K, V into blocks
2. For each block of Q:
   - Load into shared memory
   - For each block of K, V:
     - Load into shared memory
     - Compute attention for this block pair
     - Accumulate results
3. No N×N matrix ever stored
```

This achieves the same result while reducing memory from O(N²) to O(N), enabling much longer sequences.

**Gradient Checkpointing:**

Store only a subset of activations during the forward pass, recomputing the others as needed during backpropagation:

```
Standard: Store all activations (high memory, fast backward)
Checkpointing: Store only every k-th layer (low memory, slower backward)
```

This trades compute for memory. Recomputation typically adds 20-30% to training time but can reduce memory by 50-70%, enabling larger models or batch sizes.

**Mixed Precision Training:**

Use FP16 for most operations while maintaining FP32 master weights:

```
1. Forward pass: FP16
2. Compute loss: FP16
3. Backward pass: FP16 gradients
4. Update: FP16 gradients → FP32 master weights
5. Copy: FP32 → FP16 working weights
```

Dynamic loss scaling prevents underflow of small gradients. This approach provides ~2x speedup on modern GPUs while maintaining training stability.

**Tensor Parallelism:**

Split large matrices across GPUs to enable models that don't fit on a single device:

```
For weight matrix W of size [H, H] split across 2 GPUs:
GPU 1: W[:, :H/2]
GPU 2: W[:, H/2:]
```

Each GPU computes part of the matrix multiply, then results are combined via all-reduce operations.

**Pipeline Parallelism:**

Assign different layers to different GPUs:
```
GPU 1: Layers 1-8
GPU 2: Layers 9-16
GPU 3: Layers 17-24
```

Micro-batches flow through the pipeline to keep all GPUs utilized. GPipe and other schedules minimize pipeline bubbles (idle time).

**Key Papers:**
- "FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention" (Dao et al., 2022)
- "FlashAttention-2: Faster Attention with Better Parallelism" (Dao, 2023)
- "Megatron-LM: Training Multi-Billion Parameter Language Models" (Shoeybi et al., 2020)

### 4.4 Supervised Finetuning (SFT)

After pretraining, supervised finetuning adapts models to follow instructions and perform specific tasks using high-quality labeled examples.

**Data Collection:**

SFT requires datasets of input-output pairs or conversations. Sources include:

**Human-Written Examples:** Hired annotators create demonstrations of desired behavior for various tasks, ensuring diversity across domains and difficulty levels, and quality control through multiple reviews.

**Existing Datasets:** Academic datasets (SQUAD, GLUE, etc.) adapted to instruction format, dialogue datasets from customer service or assistants, and task-specific corpora.

**Synthetic Data:** Use existing strong models to generate examples, filter and verify quality, potentially iterate with human refinement.

**Training Procedure:**

Continue training the pretrained model on demonstration data using the standard language modeling objective:

```
For each example (instruction, output):
  Compute loss only on the output tokens
  Backpropagate and update parameters
```

Crucially, loss is masked on the instruction tokens—we only train the model to predict the desired output given the instruction.

**Example - Instruction Format:**

```
Instruction: Summarize the following article in 2-3 sentences.

Article: [long article text]

Response: [2-3 sentence summary]
```

The model learns to generate appropriate responses conditioned on instructions.

**Hyperparameters:**

SFT typically uses smaller learning rates than pretraining (1e-5 to 5e-5 vs 3e-4), fewer epochs (1-3 epochs over the SFT dataset), and smaller batch sizes relative to the dataset size. Too much finetuning causes overfitting and loss of general capabilities.

**Multi-Task Learning:**

Training on diverse tasks simultaneously helps the model generalize:

```
- Classification tasks
- Question answering
- Summarization
- Translation
- Code generation
- Reasoning problems
```

The model learns the common structure of instruction following rather than memorizing specific tasks.

**Data Quality Over Quantity:**

Research shows that thousands of high-quality examples often suffice for strong instruction-following. The Alpaca project demonstrated competitive performance using just 52k instruction-following examples. Quality factors include clarity of instructions, correctness of responses, diversity of tasks and formats, and appropriate difficulty levels.

**Key Papers:**
- "Finetuned Language Models are Zero-Shot Learners" (FLAN, Wei et al., 2022)
- "Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback" (InstructGPT, Ouyang et al., 2022)
- "Self-Instruct: Aligning LMs with Self-Generated Instructions" (Wang et al., 2023)

### 4.5 Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (LoRA)

Parameter-efficient methods update only a small fraction of parameters during finetuning, dramatically reducing memory and compute requirements while maintaining performance.

**Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA):**

Instead of updating all parameters in a weight matrix W, LoRA freezes W and learns a low-rank decomposition:

```
W_new = W_frozen + B·A

where:
- W is d×d (frozen)
- B is d×r  
- A is r×d
- r << d (typically r=8 to 64)
```

This reduces trainable parameters from d² to d·r·2. For d=4096 and r=8, the reduction is 4096²/(4096·8·2) = 256x fewer parameters.

**Forward Pass:**

```
h = W·x + B·A·x
  = W·x + ΔW·x
```

The LoRA matrices B·A represent the task-specific adaptation, while W contains pretrained knowledge.

**Benefits:**

LoRA offers several advantages. It enables finetuning on consumer GPUs by requiring far less memory. Multiple LoRA adapters can be trained for different tasks while sharing the base model. Adapters can be merged into the base weights for deployment. Training is faster due to fewer parameters to update. The original model is preserved, allowing easy switching between tasks.

**Example - Memory Comparison:**

For a 7B parameter model:
```
Full finetuning: ~60 GB GPU memory (model + optimizer states)
LoRA (r=8): ~15 GB GPU memory (4x reduction)
QLoRA (4-bit + LoRA): ~6 GB GPU memory (10x reduction)
```

**Rank Selection:**

The rank r trades off between expressivity and efficiency:
```
- r=1: Very efficient but limited expressivity
- r=4-8: Good balance for many tasks
- r=16-64: Higher quality for complex tasks
- r=128+: Approaching full finetuning
```

Empirically, surprisingly low ranks (r=8) often achieve performance close to full finetuning.

**QLoRA:**

Combines quantization with LoRA for maximum efficiency:

```
1. Quantize base model to 4-bit
2. Add LoRA adapters in 16-bit
3. Finetune only the adapters
```

This enables finetuning 65B models on a single 48GB GPU, democratizing access to large model customization.

**LoRA Variants:**

**AdaLoRA:** Adaptively allocates rank budget across layers based on importance.

**LoRA-FA:** Freezes one of the low-rank matrices after initialization.

**DoRA:** Decomposes weights into magnitude and direction, applying LoRA to the directional component.

**Key Papers:**
- "LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models" (Hu et al., 2022)
- "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs" (Dettmers et al., 2023)
- "AdaLoRA: Adaptive Budget Allocation" (Zhang et al., 2023)

## Part 5: LLM Tuning

### 5.1 Preference Tuning

Preference tuning aligns models with human preferences by learning from comparisons rather than demonstrations. This addresses the challenge that humans can often recognize good outputs more easily than they can produce them.

**Motivation:**

Supervised finetuning teaches models what humans demonstrate, but human demonstrations may not represent ideal behavior due to limitations in what annotators can produce, difficulty in comprehensively covering all scenarios, and quality variations across annotators.

Preference tuning sidesteps this by asking humans which of two outputs is better, a task humans excel at even when they couldn't produce either output themselves.

**Preference Data Collection:**

For each prompt:
```
1. Generate multiple candidate responses (typically 2-8)
2. Present pairs to human labelers
3. Collect preferences: "Response A is better than Response B"
4. Aggregate into preference rankings
```

This yields a dataset of tuples: (prompt, chosen_response, rejected_response).

**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO):**

DPO directly optimizes for preferences without requiring a separate reward model. The loss function encourages the model to increase the likelihood of preferred outputs while decreasing the likelihood of dispreferred outputs:

```
L_DPO = -E[log σ(β log(π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)) 
              - β log(π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x)))]

where:
- y_w: preferred (winning) output
- y_l: dispreferred (losing) output
- π_θ: policy being trained
- π_ref: reference policy (often the SFT model)
- β: temperature parameter controlling deviation from reference
```

This formulation has several advantages: no reward model training required, simpler to implement than RLHF, more stable training dynamics, and often better empirical performance.

**Identity Preference Optimization (IPO):**

A variant of DPO that modifies the loss to improve robustness:

```
L_IPO = (log(π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)) 
         - log(π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x)) - 1)²
```

The squared loss provides different optimization dynamics and can be more stable in some settings.

**Key Papers:**
- "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" (Rafailov et al., 2023)
- "A General Theoretical Paradigm to Understand Learning from Human Preferences" (Azar et al., 2023)

### 5.2 RLHF Overview

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback optimizes language models using human preferences as a reward signal, enabling models to learn complex behaviors difficult to capture through demonstration alone.

**Three-Stage Pipeline:**

**Stage 1 - Supervised Finetuning:** Train on demonstration data to get a baseline instruction-following model. This provides a reasonable starting point for RL.

**Stage 2 - Reward Modeling:** Train a model to predict human preferences, creating an automated reward signal for RL training.

**Stage 3 - RL Optimization:** Use the reward model to train the policy via reinforcement learning, improving the model based on predicted human preferences.

**Why RLHF?:**

Human values and preferences are complex and multifaceted: helpfulness, harmlessness, honesty, relevance, conciseness. These qualities are easier to judge than to define explicitly or demonstrate comprehensively. RLHF allows models to optimize for these preferences directly.

**Challenges:**

RLHF presents several difficulties. Training can be unstable due to high-variance gradients. Reward hacking occurs when models exploit flaws in the reward model. Mode collapse happens when the policy loses diversity. Distribution shift occurs when the policy diverges from the reward model's training distribution.

Various techniques address these issues: KL penalty to prevent excessive deviation from the reference policy, value function baselines to reduce variance, reward model ensembles to improve robustness, and rejection sampling to maintain quality.

**Key Papers:**
- "Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback" (Ouyang et al., 2022)
- "Learning to Summarize from Human Feedback" (Stiennon et al., 2020)

### 5.3 Reward Modeling

The reward model learns to score outputs according to human preferences, providing the training signal for reinforcement learning.

**Training Data:**

Collect comparison data where humans rank model outputs:

```
Prompt: "Write a poem about nature"

Output A: [short, simplistic poem]
Output B: [longer, more sophisticated poem]

Human preference: B > A (score: 1-10 scale)
```

Preferences can be binary (better/worse), ranked (sorting multiple outputs), or scalar (rating each output independently).

**Reward Model Architecture:**

Typically, start from the SFT model and add a scalar reward head:

```
Input: Prompt + Response
Encoder: Pretrained LLM (frozen or finetuned)
Head: Linear layer → Single scalar reward
```

**Training Objective:**

For pairwise preferences, maximize the probability that the chosen response receives a higher reward:

```
L = -E[log σ(r_θ(x, y_w) - r_θ(x, y_l))]

where:
- r_θ: reward model
- y_w: preferred response
- y_l: dispreferred response
- σ: sigmoid function
```

**Ensemble Methods:**

Training multiple reward models with different initializations or architectures provides robustness:

```
r_ensemble = mean([r_1, r_2, ..., r_k])
```

This reduces the risk of reward hacking by making it harder to exploit specific model failures.

**Validation:**

Reward model quality is assessed through agreement with held-out human preferences (typically 70-80% accuracy), generalization to distribution shifts, and calibration between predicted and actual preference probabilities.

**Failure Modes:**

Common issues include overfitting to specific preference patterns, exploitable shortcuts in the model, style biases (preferring longer or more formal text regardless of quality), and brittleness to adversarial examples.

**Key Papers:**
- "Learning to Summarize from Human Feedback" (Stiennon et al., 2020)
- "Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with RLHF" (Bai et al., 2022)

### 5.4 RL Approaches - PPO and Variants

Proximal Policy Optimization and related algorithms enable stable policy learning despite the challenges of optimizing language models with RL.

**PPO Algorithm:**

PPO optimizes a clipped objective that prevents too-large policy updates:

```
L^CLIP = E[min(r_t(θ)Â_t, 
               clip(r_t(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε)Â_t)]

where:
- r_t(θ) = π_θ(a_t|s_t) / π_θ_old(a_t|s_t) (probability ratio)
- Â_t: advantage estimate
- ε: clipping threshold (typically 0.2)
```

The clipping prevents the new policy from deviating too much from the old policy in a single update, stabilizing training.

**KL Penalty:**

Add a penalty for divergence from a reference policy:

```
Objective = E[reward(x, y)] - β·KL(π_θ || π_ref)
```

This prevents the policy from straying too far from the original model, avoiding mode collapse and maintaining general capabilities. The coefficient β balances reward maximization and similarity to the reference.

**Advantage Estimation:**

Advantages measure how much better an action is than expected:

```
Advantage = Reward - Baseline

Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE):
Â_t = Σ (γλ)^i δ_{t+i}
where δ_t = r_t + γV(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)
```

GAE balances bias and variance in advantage estimates through the parameter λ.

**PPO for Language Models:**

Adapting PPO to language generation involves treating each token as an action and the generated text as a trajectory. The reward is given at the end of the sequence (sparse reward). Training uses batches of prompts to generate completions, computing rewards and advantages, then performing multiple PPO update epochs.

**Example Training Loop:**

```
1. Sample prompts from training set
2. Generate completions using current policy
3. Score completions with reward model
4. Compute advantages using value network
5. Update policy using PPO for K epochs
6. Update value network to predict returns
7. Repeat
```

**Variants:**

**PPO-ptx:** Adds pretraining distribution matching to prevent forgetting:
```
Objective = PPO_objective + α·E[log π(x|prompt_pretrain)]
```

**Rejection Sampling:** Generate multiple samples, train on only the highest-rewarded ones. Simpler than PPO but can waste computation.

**Best-of-N:** Sample N completions at inference time, return the highest-rewarded. No training required but N times slower.

**Key Papers:**
- "Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms" (Schulman et al., 2017)
- "Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback" (Ouyang et al., 2022)
- "The N+ Implementation Details of RLHF with PPO" (Huang et al., 2024)

## Part 6: LLM Reasoning

### 6.1 Reasoning Models

Modern reasoning models use extended inference-time computation to improve performance on complex problems, trading compute for better answers.

**Test-Time Compute Scaling:**

Traditional models use fixed compute per problem. Reasoning models adaptively spend more compute on harder problems:

```
Simple problem: 1 reasoning step, 50 tokens
Medium problem: 5 reasoning steps, 200 tokens
Hard problem: 20 reasoning steps, 1000 tokens
```

This better matches human problem-solving, where we spend more time on difficult questions.

**OpenAI o1 Approach:**

While details are limited, o1 appears to use several techniques:

**Extended Reasoning:** Generate long chains of thought before producing final answers, sometimes thousands of tokens of internal reasoning. This allows breaking down complex problems into manageable steps.

**Search-Based Methods:** Explore multiple reasoning paths, backtracking when hitting dead ends. This resembles tree search or beam search over reasoning chains.

**Verification:** Check intermediate steps and final answers for consistency and correctness, using the model itself or auxiliary verifiers.

**Self-Critique:** Generate initial attempts, criticize them, and refine based on identified flaws.

**Mathematical Reasoning:**

For problems like "Prove that √2 is irrational," reasoning models might:

```
1. Recall definition of rational numbers
2. Set up proof by contradiction
3. Assume √2 = p/q in lowest terms
4. Derive 2q² = p²
5. Show p must be even
6. Show q must be even
7. Identify contradiction with lowest terms
8. Conclude √2 is irrational
```

Each step builds on previous ones, with the model verifying logical consistency throughout.

**Code Reasoning:**

When generating code, reasoning models might:

```
1. Understand the problem requirements
2. Plan the algorithm at a high level
3. Consider edge cases
4. Implement step by step
5. Test on example inputs
6. Debug if tests fail
7. Optimize if needed
```

This structured approach improves correctness and efficiency.

**Limitations:**

Current reasoning models face several challenges: they're computationally expensive (10-100x more tokens than standard models), success rates remain below expert human performance on the hardest problems, reasoning quality isn't always transparent to users, and performance on simple problems sometimes degrades due to overthinking.

**Key Papers:**
- "Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems" (Cobbe et al., 2021)
- "Let's Verify Step by Step" (Lightman et al., 2023)
- "System 2 Attention" (Weston & Sukhbaatar, 2023)

### 6.2 RL for Reasoning

Reinforcement learning proves particularly effective for training reasoning models by providing rewards for correct answers while allowing flexible reasoning paths.

**Outcome-Based Rewards:**

For problems with verifiable answers (math, code), reward correctness:

```
Reward = {
  +1 if final answer is correct
   0 if incorrect
}
```

This allows the model to discover effective reasoning strategies without needing supervision on every intermediate step.

**Process-Based Rewards:**

Rather than only rewarding final answers, reward each reasoning step:

```
For each step i:
  r_i = P(step_i leads to correct answer)
```

This provides denser feedback, helping the model learn which reasoning patterns work. However, it requires training a process reward model or human labeling of reasoning steps.

**Self-Play and Verification:**

The model can improve by:

```
1. Generate multiple solution attempts
2. Verify each attempt
3. Train on solutions that verify correctly
4. Use failed attempts as negative examples
```

This creates a self-improvement loop where the model learns from its successes and failures.

**Example - Math Problem:**

Problem: "If 2x + 5 = 17, what is x?"

Reasoning process with rewards:
```
Step 1: "Subtract 5 from both sides" → r=0.9 (good step)
Step 2: "2x = 12" → r=0.95 (correct algebra)
Step 3: "Divide by 2" → r=0.98 (good step)
Step 4: "x = 6" → r=1.0 (correct answer)
```

The model learns that this reasoning chain reliably leads to correct answers.

**Key Papers:**
- "Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems" (Cobbe et al., 2021)
- "Let's Verify Step by Step" (Lightman et al., 2023)
- "Solving Math Word Problems with Process Supervised RM" (Uesato et al., 2022)

### 6.3 GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)

GRPO improves upon standard PPO by computing advantages relative to other samples in the same batch, providing better reward signal normalization.

**Core Idea:**

Instead of comparing each sample to a learned value function, compare it to other samples for the same prompt:

```
Advantage_i = Reward_i - mean(Rewards for same prompt)
```

This provides several benefits: it automatically handles reward scale variations across prompts, reduces the need for careful value function training, and improves training stability by normalizing within context.

**Algorithm:**

```
For each prompt in batch:
  1. Generate K completions
  2. Compute rewards for all K
  3. Calculate group mean: μ = mean(rewards)
  4. Compute advantages: A_i = R_i - μ
  5. Update policy using PPO with these advantages
```

**Advantages:**

GRPO offers practical benefits: simpler implementation than standard PPO with value functions, better handling of reward distribution shifts, improved sample efficiency by leveraging multiple samples per prompt, and reduced hyperparameter sensitivity.

**Example:**

Prompt: "Explain photosynthesis"

Generate 4 completions:
```
Completion 1: Reward = 7.5 → Advantage = +0.5
Completion 2: Reward = 8.2 → Advantage = +1.2
Completion 3: Reward = 6.8 → Advantage = -0.2
Completion 4: Reward = 6.5 → Advantage = -1.5
Mean reward = 7.0
```

The policy is pushed toward the better completions (1, 2) and away from worse ones (3, 4), all relative to this specific prompt's difficulty.

**Key Papers:**
- "Group Relative Policy Optimization" (Shao et al., 2024)
- "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning" (Shao et al., 2024)

### 6.4 Scaling

Test-time and training-time scaling represent two complementary approaches to improving model capabilities.

**Training-Time Scaling:**

Traditional scaling increases model size, training data, and compute during the training phase:

```
Training compute ∝ Parameters × Tokens × FLOPs per parameter
```

This follows predictable scaling laws, allowing forecasting of larger models' performance. However, benefits plateau without proportional increases in all dimensions, and costs grow superlinearly with model size.

**Test-Time Scaling:**

Reasoning models scale inference compute by generating more tokens, exploring multiple solutions, performing verification steps, and iterating on answers.

This offers different tradeoffs: costs scale per query rather than amortized across all future queries, allows adaptive compute based on problem difficulty, and provides immediate benefits without retraining.

**Best-of-N Scaling:**

Generate N samples and return the best:

```
Performance ∝ log(N) for well-calibrated reward models
```

Empirically, Best-of-64 can improve pass rates on coding problems by 20-40% over single samples. Diminishing returns appear beyond N=100-1000 depending on the task.

**Iterative Refinement:**

Generate initial answer, critique it, refine based on critique, and repeat. Each iteration can improve quality, though returns diminish after 2-3 iterations for most tasks.

**Scaling Laws for Reasoning:**

Preliminary results suggest:

```
Performance ∝ (Training Compute)^a + (Test Compute)^b

where a, b depend on the task
```

For some problems, test-time scaling can be more cost-effective than training larger models, especially when queries are infrequent.

**Example - Code Generation:**

Generate 1 sample: 60% pass rate, cost = 1x
Generate 10 samples (best-of-10): 75% pass rate, cost = 10x
Generate 64 samples (best-of-64): 82% pass rate, cost = 64x

The optimal N depends on the value of success versus the cost of computation.

**Key Papers:**
- "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (Hoffmann et al., 2022)
- "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization" (Gao et al., 2023)
- "Large Language Monkeys: Scaling Inference Compute" (Bradley et al., 2024)

## Part 7: Agentic LLMs

### 7.1 Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

RAG enhances language models by retrieving relevant information from external knowledge sources, addressing the limitations of purely parametric knowledge.

**Motivation:**

LLMs have limitations: parametric knowledge is fixed at training time, they struggle with rare or recent information, they cannot cite sources for claims, and updating knowledge requires expensive retraining.

RAG addresses these by augmenting generation with retrieved documents from up-to-date knowledge bases, private or domain-specific information, and citations for verification.

**Basic RAG Pipeline:**

```
1. Query: User asks a question
2. Retrieval: Find relevant documents from knowledge base
3. Augmentation: Concatenate query + retrieved docs
4. Generation: LLM generates answer using retrieved context
```

**Retrieval Methods:**

**Sparse Retrieval (BM25):** Traditional information retrieval using term frequency and inverse document frequency:

```
BM25(q, d) = Σ IDF(q_i) · (f(q_i, d) · (k+1)) / 
                           (f(q_i, d) + k · (1-b + b · |d|/avgdl))
```

Fast and effective for keyword matching, but struggles with semantic similarity.

**Dense Retrieval:** Encode queries and documents into vector space using embedding models:

```
Similarity(q, d) = cosine(embed(q), embed(d))
```

Better captures semantic relationships but requires training embedding models and building vector indices.

**Hybrid Retrieval:** Combine sparse and dense methods:

```
Score = α · BM25_score + (1-α) · Dense_score
```

This leverages both keyword matching and semantic understanding.

**Example:**

Query: "What is the capital of France?"

Retrieved documents:
```
Doc 1 (score: 0.95): "Paris is the capital and largest city of France..."
Doc 2 (score: 0.87): "France is a country in Western Europe. Its capital is Paris..."
Doc 3 (score: 0.45): "Lyon is the third-largest city in France..."
```

Augmented prompt:
```
Context: [Doc 1 and Doc 2 text]

Question: What is the capital of France?
Answer: Based on the provided context, Paris is the capital of France.
```

**Reranking:**

After initial retrieval, rerank documents using a more sophisticated model:

```
1. Retrieve top 100 documents (fast, approximate)
2. Rerank using cross-encoder (slow, accurate)
3. Use top 5 for generation
```

Cross-encoders encode query and document together, capturing fine-grained relevance signals.

**Key Papers:**
- "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (Lewis et al., 2020)
- "Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain QA" (Karpukhin et al., 2020)
- "REALM: Retrieval-Augmented Language Model Pre-Training" (Guu et al., 2020)

### 7.2 Advanced RAG Techniques

Moving beyond basic retrieval-augmentation, advanced techniques address specific challenges and improve performance.

**Query Transformation:**

Rewrite user queries to improve retrieval:

**Query Expansion:** Add related terms or rephrase:
```
Original: "LLM training"
Expanded: "Large language model training, neural network pretraining, transformer model optimization"
```

**Step-Back Prompting:** Generate broader context queries:
```
Specific: "How do I fix error X in PyTorch?"
Step-back: "What are common PyTorch errors and debugging approaches?"
```

**Multi-Query:** Generate multiple query variations:
```
Query: "Best practices for RAG"
Variations: [
  "How to build effective RAG systems",
  "Common RAG implementation challenges",
  "Optimizing retrieval-augmented generation"
]
```

**Recursive Retrieval:**

Use initial retrieved documents to inform subsequent retrievals:

```
1. Retrieve based on original query
2. Extract key entities/concepts from retrieved docs
3. Perform follow-up retrievals based on these
4. Combine all retrieved information
```

**Hierarchical Retrieval:**

Structure knowledge at multiple granularities:

```
Document → Sections → Paragraphs → Sentences
```

First retrieve relevant documents, then zoom into relevant sections, then extract specific sentences.

**Contextual Compression:**

Retrieved documents often contain irrelevant information. Compression extracts only relevant portions:

```
1. Retrieve full documents
2. Use LLM to extract relevant excerpts
3. Pass compressed context to generation
```

This improves context utilization and reduces prompt length.

**Hypothetical Document Embeddings (HyDE):**

Generate hypothetical answer documents and use them for retrieval:

```
1. Query: "What causes rainbows?"
2. LLM generates hypothetical answer
3. Embed and use this to retrieve actual documents
4. Generate final answer from retrieved docs
```

This works because hypothetical answers match the style and terminology of relevant documents better than queries.

**Self-RAG:**

The model decides when to retrieve and how to use retrieved information:

```
1. Generate initial response
2. Model determines if retrieval would help
3. If yes, retrieve and incorporate information
4. Model assesses if response is now supported
5. Iterate if needed
```

**Key Papers:**
- "Self-RAG: Learning to Retrieve, Generate, and Critique" (Asai et al., 2023)
- "Query Rewriting for Retrieval-Augmented LLMs" (Ma et al., 2023)
- "Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels" (HyDE, Gao et al., 2023)

### 7.3 Function Calling

Function calling enables language models to interact with external tools and APIs, extending their capabilities beyond text generation.

**Mechanism:**

The model is trained to output structured function calls when appropriate:

```
User: "What's the weather in Paris?"

Model output:
<function_call>
  name: get_weather
  arguments: {"location": "Paris, France"}
</function_call>

System executes function → Returns: {"temp": 18, "conditions": "partly cloudy"}

Model: "The weather in Paris is 18°C and partly cloudy."
```

**Function Definitions:**

Provide the model with available functions and their schemas:

```json
{
  "name": "get_weather",
  "description": "Get current weather for a location",
  "parameters": {
    "type": "object",
    "properties": {
      "location": {
        "type": "string",
        "description": "City and country, e.g. Paris, France"
      }
    },
    "required": ["location"]
  }
}
```

**Training:**

Models learn function calling through:

**Synthetic Data:** Generate training examples of queries and corresponding function calls.

**Human Demonstrations:** Annotators provide examples of when and how to call functions.

**Reinforcement Learning:** Reward successful function use and penalize incorrect calls.

**Multi-Step Function Calling:**

Complex tasks may require multiple function calls:

```
User: "Book me a flight to Tokyo and find a hotel nearby"

Step 1: search_flights(destination="Tokyo")
Step 2: book_flight(flight_id=...)
Step 3: search_hotels(location="Tokyo", checkin=...)
Step 4: Format response with booking confirmations
```

**Parallel Function Calling:**

Execute independent functions simultaneously:

```
User: "What's the weather in London and New York?"

Parallel calls:
- get_weather(location="London, UK")
- get_weather(location="New York, USA")

Combine results in response
```

**Error Handling:**

Models must gracefully handle function failures:

```
If function returns error:
  - Acknowledge the issue
  - Try alternative approaches
  - Inform user if task impossible
```

**Key Papers:**
- "Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools" (Schick et al., 2023)
- "Gorilla: Large Language Model Connected with Massive APIs" (Patil et al., 2023)

### 7.4 Agents

LLM agents autonomously pursue goals by iterating over observation, thought, action cycles, using tools and memory to accomplish complex tasks.

**Agent Architecture:**

```
1. Perceive: Observe environment state
2. Plan: Decide what to do
3. Act: Execute actions
4. Reflect: Learn from outcomes
5. Repeat until goal achieved
```

**Memory Systems:**

**Short-term Memory:** Recent conversation context, maintained in the prompt or context window.

**Long-term Memory:** Persistent information across sessions, stored in vector databases or knowledge graphs, retrieved when relevant.

**Example - Research Agent:**

Task: "Research the history of transformers in NLP and write a summary"

Agent's process:
```
1. Plan: [Search for papers, Read key papers, Synthesize summary]
2. Action: search_papers("transformer neural networks")
3. Observe: [List of papers and abstracts]
4. Action: read_paper("Attention Is All You Need")
5. Observe: [Paper content]
6. Action: read_paper("BERT")
7. Observe: [Paper content]
8. Reflect: "I have enough information about origins and key developments"
9. Action: write_summary()
10. Done
```

**Planning Strategies:**

**ReAct (Reasoning + Acting):** Interleave reasoning traces with actions:

```
Thought: I need to find the current CEO of Tesla
Action: search("CEO of Tesla")
Observation: Results show Elon Musk
Thought: I should verify this is current
Action: search("Tesla CEO 2024")
Observation: Confirms Elon Musk
Answer: Elon Musk is the CEO of Tesla
```

**Plan-and-Execute:** Generate complete plan, then execute steps:

```
Plan:
1. Search for company financial data
2. Extract revenue figures
3. Calculate growth rate
4. Format findings

Execute each step sequentially
```

**Tool Use:**

Agents have access to various tools: web search and browsing, calculators for math, code interpreters, databases and APIs, and file systems.

**Multi-Agent Systems:**

Multiple specialized agents collaborate:

```
Researcher Agent: Gathers information
Analyst Agent: Analyzes data
Writer Agent: Synthesizes reports
Critic Agent: Reviews and provides feedback
```

Each agent focuses on its specialty, improving overall system capability.

**Key Papers:**
- "ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models" (Yao et al., 2023)
- "Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior" (Park et al., 2023)
- "AutoGPT" (Significant Labs, 2023)

### 7.5 ReAct Framework

ReAct combines reasoning and acting by prompting models to generate explicit reasoning traces alongside actions, improving interpretability and performance.

**Core Pattern:**

```
Thought: [Reasoning about what to do next]
Action: [Action to take]
Observation: [Result of action]
... (repeat)
Thought: I now know the final answer
Final Answer: [Answer to original question]
```

**Example - Multi-Hop Question:**

Question: "What is the population of the capital of France?"

ReAct trace:
```
Thought 1: I need to first find the capital of France
Action 1: search("capital of France")
Observation 1: Paris is the capital of France

Thought 2: Now I need to find the population of Paris
Action 2: search("population of Paris")
Observation 2: The population of Paris is approximately 2.2 million

Thought 3: I now have the answer
Final Answer: The population of Paris, the capital of France, is approximately 2.2 million
```

**Benefits:**

The explicit reasoning provides several advantages. It enables better debugging through visible decision-making. Models can recover from errors by reasoning about observations. Humans can verify the reasoning process. The approach generalizes better to new tasks through compositional reasoning.

**Training:**

ReAct behavior can be elicited through few-shot prompting with examples, finetuning on ReAct traces (synthetic or human-generated), or reinforcement learning rewarding successful reasoning-action sequences.

**Comparison to Alternatives:**

**Pure Acting (no reasoning):** Faster but less interpretable and struggles with complex multi-step problems.

**Pure Reasoning (no actions):** Cannot access external information or tools.

**ReAct:** Combines benefits of both at the cost of longer generation.

**Limitations:**

ReAct faces some challenges. Reasoning can be incorrect even when well-articulated. Verbose traces increase token usage and latency. Models sometimes generate unproductive reasoning loops. Evaluation requires assessing both reasoning quality and task success.

**Key Papers:**
- "ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models" (Yao et al., 2023)
- "Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning" (Shinn et al., 2023)

## Part 8: Evaluation of LLMs

### 8.1 LLM-as-a-Judge Overview

Using LLMs to evaluate other LLMs' outputs has become increasingly practical as models improve, offering a scalable alternative to human evaluation.

**Motivation:**

Human evaluation is the gold standard but has limitations: it's expensive and time-consuming, difficult to scale, suffers from annotator disagreement, and lacks reproducibility.

LLM judges can evaluate many examples quickly, provide consistent criteria application, scale to large datasets, and enable rapid iteration.

**Basic Setup:**

```
Prompt to judge:
"You are evaluating assistant responses for helpfulness and accuracy.

Question: [original question]
Response: [model's response]

Rate the response from 1-10 on:
- Helpfulness
- Accuracy
- Clarity

Provide your rating and justification."

Judge output: [Scores and reasoning]
```

**Judging Strategies:**

**Single Score:** Assign an overall quality score:
```
"Rate this response from 1-10: [score]"
```

**Multi-Aspect:** Evaluate multiple dimensions separately:
```
Helpfulness: 8/10
Accuracy: 9/10
Safety: 10/10
```

**Pairwise Comparison:** Judge which of two responses is better:
```
Response A: [text]
Response B: [text]

Which response is better? A/B/Tie
```

**Reference-Based:** Compare against a reference answer:
```
Reference: [gold answer]
Candidate: [model answer]

How well does the candidate match the reference?
```

**Example - Pairwise Evaluation:**

Question: "Explain photosynthesis simply"

Response A: "Photosynthesis is when plants use sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide to make food and oxygen."

Response B: "Photosynthesis involves chloroplasts capturing photons which excite electrons in photosystem II, initiating the light-dependent reactions..."

Judge: "Response A is better. While Response B is more detailed, Response A better addresses the request for a simple explanation."

**Key Papers:**
- "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena" (Zheng et al., 2023)
- "LLM-as-a-Judge: Aligned with Human Judgment" (Bai et al., 2023)

### 8.2 Best Practices and Benefits

Effective use of LLM judges requires careful design of evaluation protocols and awareness of the approach's strengths.

**Protocol Design:**

**Clear Rubrics:** Provide explicit evaluation criteria:

```
Evaluate on a scale of 1-5:
1: Completely incorrect or unhelpful
2: Partially correct but major issues
3: Acceptable with some problems
4: Good with minor issues
5: Excellent, comprehensive and accurate
```

**Detailed Instructions:** Specify what to consider:

```
When evaluating, consider:
- Factual accuracy
- Relevance to the question
- Clarity of explanation
- Completeness of coverage
- Appropriate level of detail
```

**Few-Shot Examples:** Show example evaluations:

```
Example 1:
Question: [Q1]
Response: [R1]
Evaluation: Score 4 - Response is accurate and clear but misses [specific aspect]

Example 2:
Question: [Q2]
Response: [R2]
Evaluation: Score 2 - Contains factual errors about [topic]
```

**Benefits:**

LLM judges offer several advantages:

**Consistency:** Unlike humans, LLM judges apply the same criteria uniformly across all evaluations, reducing subjective variation.

**Scalability:** Can evaluate thousands of responses in minutes rather than weeks.

**Cost-Effectiveness:** Dramatically cheaper than human annotation at scale.

**Rapid Iteration:** Enables fast experimentation with different model versions or prompting strategies.

**Granular Feedback:** Can provide detailed explanations for scores, helpful for model improvement.

**Multi-Lingual:** Strong models can evaluate content in many languages without requiring multilingual annotators.

**Best Practices:**

For effective LLM judging: use strong models (GPT-4, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, or better) as judges, validate against human judgments on a subset, use multiple judges or multiple samples for important decisions, calibrate judge models on your specific domain, randomize order in pairwise comparisons to detect position bias, and provide task-specific rubrics rather than generic ones.

**Key Papers:**
- "Can Large Language Models Be an Alternative to Human Evaluation?" (Zheng et al., 2024)

### 8.3 Biases and Pitfalls

LLM judges exhibit systematic biases that can compromise evaluation validity if not addressed.

**Position Bias:**

Judges tend to prefer responses in certain positions:

```
Comparison: (A vs B)
Judge prefers A: 60%

Comparison: (B vs A) - same responses, swapped order
Judge prefers B: 55%
```

The same response gets different evaluations depending on its position. This affects pairwise comparisons significantly.

**Mitigation:** Evaluate both orderings and average, or use multiple judges with randomized positions.

**Length Bias:**

Judges often favor longer responses regardless of quality:

```
Short, accurate answer: 7/10
Long, verbose answer with similar content: 8.5/10
```

This particularly affects comparisons where one model tends to generate longer outputs.

**Mitigation:** Explicitly instruct judges to value conciseness, normalize scores by length, or use length-stratified evaluation.

**Self-Preference Bias:**

Models rate their own outputs higher than others':

```
GPT-4 judging:
GPT-4 response: 8.5/10
Claude response: 7.5/10

Claude judging:
GPT-4 response: 7.8/10
Claude response: 8.3/10
```

**Mitigation:** Use third-party judges (neither model being evaluated), ensemble multiple different judge models, or validate against human preferences.

**Style Bias:**

Judges prefer certain writing styles or formats:

```
Formal, structured response: 9/10
Casual but accurate response: 7/10
```

This may not align with user preferences or task requirements.

**Mitigation:** Calibrate judges on diverse style examples, explicitly instruct style-agnostic evaluation, or use task-specific style criteria.

**Verbosity vs. Quality:**

Judges can confuse detailed elaboration with quality:

```
Response with extra context: "Paris is the capital of France. Founded in the 3rd century BC, it's known for the Eiffel Tower..."
Concise response: "Paris"

For question "What's the capital of France?", the concise answer may be better but score lower.
```

**Agreement with Humans:**

LLM judges don't always align with human preferences:

```
Human agreement rate: 70-85% depending on task
Perfect agreement: rarely achieved
Systematic disagreements: common in subjective domains
```

**Mitigation:** Regularly validate against human judgments, identify systematic disagreement patterns, and adjust rubrics or judge models accordingly.

**Key Papers:**
- "Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators" (Wang et al., 2023)
- "The Wisdom of Hindsight Makes Language Models Better Instruction Followers" (Zhang et al., 2023)

### 8.4 Vision-Language Models as Judges

Extending LLM-as-a-judge to multimodal content enables evaluation of image generation, video understanding, and visual reasoning tasks.

**Multimodal Evaluation:**

VLMs can assess outputs requiring vision understanding:

```
Task: Generate an image of "a red car on a beach"

Evaluation prompt:
Image: [generated image]
Text prompt: "a red car on a beach"

Does the image match the prompt? Evaluate:
- Presence of red car: Yes/No
- Beach setting: Yes/No
- Overall quality: 1-10
```

**Use Cases:**

**Image Generation Evaluation:** Assess text-to-image models for prompt adherence, image quality, style consistency, and harmful content detection.

**Visual Question Answering:** Verify answers to questions about images for accuracy, completeness, and groundedness in visual evidence.

**Image Captioning:** Evaluate caption quality for accuracy, detail level, and relevance.

**Video Understanding:** Assess video descriptions, action recognition, or temporal reasoning.

**Example - Image Quality:**

```
Judge prompt:
"Evaluate this generated image on:
1. Prompt alignment (1-10)
2. Visual quality (1-10)
3. Realism (1-10)
4. Aesthetic appeal (1-10)

Prompt: 'A serene mountain landscape at sunset'
Image: [image data]

Analysis: The image shows mountains with sunset colors. The lighting is realistic and the composition is pleasing. However, some details appear slightly blurred.

Scores:
Prompt alignment: 9/10
Visual quality: 7/10
Realism: 8/10
Aesthetic appeal: 8/10"
```

**Challenges:**

VLM judges face unique difficulties: subjective aesthetic judgments are harder than text quality assessment, models may hallucinate visual details not actually present, evaluation criteria are less standardized than for text, and computational costs are higher for vision models.

**Best Practices:**

For effective VLM judging: use high-resolution inputs when possible, provide specific visual criteria rather than general quality, validate on datasets with human annotations, use multiple judges for subjective assessments, and combine automated VLM evaluation with spot-checking by humans.

**Comparison to Human Evaluation:**

VLMs show promise but limitations:
```
Agreement on objective criteria: 75-85%
Agreement on aesthetic judgments: 60-70%
Hallucination rate: 10-20% depending on model
```

For critical applications, human validation remains essential, but VLM judges can handle initial screening and routine evaluation.
