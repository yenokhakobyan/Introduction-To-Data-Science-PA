# 1. Foundations of Audio & Signal Processing

## 1.1 Sound as a Signal

### The Physical Nature of Sound

Sound is a mechanical wave—a pressure disturbance propagating through a medium (air, water, solid). When you speak, your vocal cords create pressure variations that travel outward. A microphone converts these pressure variations into an electrical signal, which we then digitize for computational processing.

**Intuition**: Think of dropping a stone into still water. The ripples spreading outward are analogous to sound waves. The height of each ripple at any point corresponds to the instantaneous pressure at that location.

### Continuous-Time vs. Discrete-Time Signals

A **continuous-time signal** x(t) is defined for every real value of time t ∈ ℝ. Physical sound is inherently continuous—pressure varies smoothly and exists at every instant.

A **discrete-time signal** x[n] is defined only at integer indices n ∈ ℤ. Computers cannot store infinite precision, so we sample the continuous signal at regular intervals.

**Mathematical Relationship**:

$$x[n] = x(nT_s)$$

where $T_s$ is the sampling period (time between samples) and $n$ is the sample index.

**Numeric Example**: If we sample at $T_s = 0.0000625$ seconds (i.e., 16,000 samples per second), then:
- x[0] = x(0 s) — the pressure at time zero
- x[1] = x(0.0000625 s) — pressure at ~63 microseconds
- x[16000] = x(1.0 s) — pressure at exactly 1 second

One second of audio at 16 kHz sampling rate gives us exactly 16,000 numbers.

### Sampling Rate and the Nyquist Theorem

The **sampling rate** (or sampling frequency) $f_s = 1/T_s$ tells us how many samples we capture per second, measured in Hertz (Hz).

**Common sampling rates in practice**:
- 8,000 Hz: Telephone-quality speech
- 16,000 Hz: Wideband speech (VoIP, voice assistants)
- 22,050 Hz: Low-quality music
- 44,100 Hz: CD-quality audio
- 48,000 Hz: Professional video/broadcast audio
- 96,000 Hz: High-resolution audio production

**The Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem** states: A continuous signal can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples if and only if the sampling rate is at least twice the highest frequency component in the signal.

$$f_s \geq 2 f_{max}$$

The frequency $f_N = f_s / 2$ is called the **Nyquist frequency**—the maximum frequency we can faithfully represent.

**Intuition**: Imagine trying to capture a spinning wheel on video. If the wheel spins once per second (1 Hz) and you film at 1 frame per second, the wheel appears stationary (you always catch it at the same position). You need at least 2 frames per second to see that it's moving. Similarly, to capture a 1000 Hz tone, you need at least 2000 samples per second.

**Why this matters**: Human hearing extends to roughly 20,000 Hz. CD audio uses 44,100 Hz because $44100/2 = 22050$ Hz exceeds our hearing range, ensuring we capture everything audible.

### Aliasing: When Sampling Goes Wrong

**Aliasing** occurs when we sample too slowly—frequencies above the Nyquist frequency "fold back" and masquerade as lower frequencies, corrupting our signal irreversibly.

**Mathematical explanation**: A frequency $f$ above Nyquist appears as $|f - k \cdot f_s|$ for some integer $k$ that brings it into the range $[0, f_N]$.

**Numeric Example**: Suppose $f_s = 8000$ Hz (Nyquist = 4000 Hz). A 5000 Hz tone will alias to $|5000 - 8000| = 3000$ Hz. We hear 3000 Hz, not 5000 Hz—and there's no way to recover the original.

**Prevention**: Before sampling, apply an **anti-aliasing filter** (a low-pass filter that removes frequencies above Nyquist). All practical ADCs (analog-to-digital converters) include this.

## 1.2 Digital Audio Representation

### Waveform as a 1D Tensor

Once digitized, audio becomes a sequence of numbers—a 1D tensor (vector) of shape **(T,)** where T is the number of samples.

**Example**: 3 seconds of audio at 16 kHz:
- T = 3 × 16000 = 48,000 samples
- Shape: `(48000,)` or equivalently `torch.Size([48000])`
- Each value represents the amplitude at that instant (typically normalized to [-1, 1])

```python
# Loading audio in Python
import torchaudio

waveform, sample_rate = torchaudio.load("speech.wav")
# waveform.shape might be torch.Size([1, 48000]) for mono
# waveform.shape might be torch.Size([2, 96000]) for stereo, 6 seconds
```

### Multi-Channel Audio: Shape (C, T)

Real-world audio often has multiple channels:
- **Mono**: 1 channel, shape (1, T)
- **Stereo**: 2 channels (left, right), shape (2, T)
- **5.1 Surround**: 6 channels, shape (6, T)
- **Microphone arrays**: Variable, e.g., (8, T) for an 8-mic array

**Convention in PyTorch/torchaudio**: Shape is **(C, T)** — channels first, then time.

**Batch dimension**: When training models, we add a batch dimension: **(B, C, T)** where B is batch size.

### Quantization and Bit Depth

**Quantization** maps continuous amplitude values to a finite set of discrete levels.

**Bit depth** determines how many levels we have:
- 8-bit: $2^8 = 256$ levels
- 16-bit: $2^{16} = 65,536$ levels (CD quality)
- 24-bit: $2^{24} = 16,777,216$ levels (professional audio)
- 32-bit float: Effectively continuous for ML purposes

**Quantization noise** is the error introduced by rounding to discrete levels. The **Signal-to-Quantization-Noise Ratio (SQNR)** improves by roughly 6 dB per additional bit:

$$SQNR \approx 6.02 \times b + 1.76 \text{ dB}$$

where $b$ is bit depth.

**Numeric Example**: 
- 8-bit audio: SQNR ≈ 50 dB (noticeable hiss)
- 16-bit audio: SQNR ≈ 98 dB (effectively noise-free for human ears)

**For ML**: We almost always work with 32-bit floating point internally, normalizing to [-1, 1]. Quantization matters primarily for storage and deployment on edge devices.

## 1.3 Time-Domain vs. Frequency-Domain Representations

### Time Domain: What Happens When

In the **time domain**, we represent the signal as amplitude versus time: $x[n]$ tells us the amplitude at sample $n$.

**Strengths**: 
- Direct physical interpretation
- Preserves exact temporal structure
- Natural for transient events (clicks, onsets)

**Weaknesses**:
- Hard to see frequency content
- A 440 Hz tone and white noise look equally "wiggly"
- Difficult to separate overlapping sounds

### Frequency Domain: What Frequencies Are Present

In the **frequency domain**, we represent the signal as amplitude versus frequency: $X[k]$ tells us how much of frequency $k$ is present.

**Strengths**:
- Reveals harmonic structure (fundamental + overtones)
- Natural for music and speech analysis
- Separates frequency components clearly

**Weaknesses**:
- Loses temporal information (when did that frequency occur?)
- Requires the entire signal for analysis
- Not suitable for non-stationary signals without modification

**Key insight**: Time and frequency domains contain the same information—they're just different views. The Fourier Transform is the bridge between them.

## 1.4 The Fourier Transform

### Intuition: Decomposing into Sinusoids

The Fourier Transform decomposes any signal into a sum of sinusoids (sines and cosines) of different frequencies. It answers: "How much of each frequency is present in this signal?"

**Analogy**: Imagine you have a smoothie and want to know the ingredients. The Fourier Transform is like a magical taste analyzer that tells you "30% strawberry, 25% banana, 20% yogurt, ..." The original smoothie (time domain) and the ingredient list (frequency domain) describe the same drink differently.

### Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)

For a discrete signal $x[n]$ of length $N$, the **DFT** produces $N$ complex frequency coefficients:

$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cdot e^{-j \frac{2\pi k n}{N}}, \quad k = 0, 1, ..., N-1$$

where $j = \sqrt{-1}$ is the imaginary unit.

**Interpreting the output**:
- $X[k]$ is complex: $X[k] = a + jb$
- **Magnitude** $|X[k]| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ tells us how much of frequency $k$ is present
- **Phase** $\angle X[k] = \arctan(b/a)$ tells us the phase offset of that frequency component

**Frequency bins**: Each index $k$ corresponds to frequency $f_k = k \cdot f_s / N$ Hz.

### 8-Point DFT: A Worked Example

Let's compute the DFT of an 8-sample signal by hand. Consider:

$$x = [1, 0, -1, 0, 1, 0, -1, 0]$$

This is a simple alternating pattern—intuitively, it should have energy at a specific frequency.

**Setup**: $N = 8$, so we compute $X[k]$ for $k = 0, 1, ..., 7$.

**The DFT matrix** approach: We can write the DFT as $X = W \cdot x$ where $W$ is the DFT matrix with entries $W_{kn} = e^{-j2\pi kn/N}$.

For N=8, the fundamental complex exponential is $\omega = e^{-j2\pi/8} = e^{-j\pi/4}$.

**Computing X[0]** (DC component / average):
$$X[0] = \sum_{n=0}^{7} x[n] \cdot e^{0} = 1 + 0 - 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 - 1 + 0 = 0$$

The signal has zero mean—makes sense, it oscillates symmetrically around zero.

**Computing X[2]** (corresponds to 2 cycles over 8 samples):
$$X[2] = \sum_{n=0}^{7} x[n] \cdot e^{-j\pi n/2}$$

Working through: $e^{-j\pi n/2}$ cycles through $\{1, -j, -1, j, 1, -j, -1, j\}$ for $n = 0..7$.

$X[2] = 1(1) + 0(-j) + (-1)(-1) + 0(j) + 1(1) + 0(-j) + (-1)(-1) + 0(j)$
$X[2] = 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 = 4$

**Full result**: 
$$X = [0, 0, 4, 0, 0, 0, 4, 0]$$

**Interpretation**: All energy is at $k=2$ (and its mirror $k=6$ due to symmetry for real signals). Our signal contains exactly one frequency—2 complete cycles over 8 samples.

If $f_s = 8000$ Hz, then frequency bin $k=2$ corresponds to $f = 2 \times 8000 / 8 = 2000$ Hz.

### Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

The **FFT** is an algorithm that computes the DFT efficiently.

**Complexity**:
- Naive DFT: $O(N^2)$ — each of N outputs requires summing N terms
- FFT: $O(N \log N)$ — exploits symmetries in the DFT matrix

**Practical impact**: For N = 1,000,000 samples:
- Naive: ~$10^{12}$ operations
- FFT: ~$20 \times 10^6$ operations

That's a 50,000× speedup. Without FFT, modern audio processing would be computationally infeasible.

**The Cooley-Tukey algorithm** (most common FFT) works by recursively splitting the DFT into smaller DFTs. It works most efficiently when N is a power of 2 (256, 512, 1024, 2048, ...).

## 1.5 Windowing and Framing

### Why We Need Frames

Real audio signals are **non-stationary**—their properties change over time. Speech transitions between phonemes; music changes notes, chords, dynamics.

The DFT assumes the signal is periodic and stationary. Applying it to an entire audio file gives us "average" frequency content, losing all temporal evolution.

**Solution**: Divide the signal into short **frames** (typically 20-50 ms), assume each frame is approximately stationary, and analyze frames independently.

**Human-scale intuition**: In 25 ms, a speaker typically produces about one phoneme. The signal is "locally stationary" at this scale—short enough to be stable, long enough to contain several pitch periods.

### Frame Length and Hop Size

**Frame length** (or window size) $N_{frame}$: The number of samples in each analysis window.

**Hop size** (or hop length) $H$: The number of samples we advance between consecutive frames.

**Overlap** = $N_{frame} - H$. Common choices:
- 50% overlap: $H = N_{frame}/2$
- 75% overlap: $H = N_{frame}/4$

**Numeric Example**:
- Sample rate: 16,000 Hz
- Frame length: 400 samples = 25 ms
- Hop size: 160 samples = 10 ms
- Overlap: 240 samples = 15 ms (60% overlap)

For a 1-second clip (16,000 samples), we get approximately:
$$\text{Number of frames} = \left\lfloor \frac{16000 - 400}{160} \right\rfloor + 1 = 98 \text{ frames}$$

### Window Functions

Simply chopping the signal creates **spectral leakage**—sharp discontinuities at frame boundaries introduce artificial high frequencies.

**Window functions** taper the frame smoothly to zero at the edges. The signal is multiplied element-wise by the window:

$$x_{windowed}[n] = x[n] \cdot w[n]$$

**Common windows**:

1. **Rectangular** (no window): $w[n] = 1$. Sharp edges, maximum leakage.

2. **Hann window**: 
$$w[n] = 0.5 \left(1 - \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N-1}\right)\right)$$
Smooth, widely used, good all-around choice.

3. **Hamming window**:
$$w[n] = 0.54 - 0.46 \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N-1}\right)$$
Slightly better sidelobe suppression than Hann.

4. **Blackman window**: Even smoother, better sidelobe rejection, but wider main lobe.

**Trade-off**: Smoother windows reduce leakage but blur frequency resolution. For most audio ML, Hann is the default choice.

### The Stationarity Assumption

Within each frame, we assume the signal's statistical properties (frequency content, amplitude) are constant. This is the **local stationarity assumption**.

**Why it matters**: If the assumption is violated (frame too long, rapid changes), our frequency analysis becomes meaningless—we're averaging properties that shouldn't be averaged.

**Rule of thumb**: Frame lengths of 20-50 ms work well for speech and most audio. Music with very fast transients (drums, percussion) may benefit from shorter frames (10-20 ms).

# 2. Time–Frequency Representations

## 2.1 Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)

### Mathematical Definition

The **STFT** applies the DFT to each windowed frame, giving us frequency content as a function of time. For a signal $x[n]$:

$$X[m, k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n + mH] \cdot w[n] \cdot e^{-j\frac{2\pi kn}{N}}$$

where:
- $m$ is the frame index (time axis)
- $k$ is the frequency bin index  
- $N$ is the frame/FFT length
- $H$ is the hop size
- $w[n]$ is the window function

**Output shape**: For a signal of length $T$ samples:
- Number of frames: $M = \lfloor(T - N) / H\rfloor + 1$
- Number of frequency bins: $N/2 + 1$ (for real signals, we keep only non-negative frequencies due to conjugate symmetry)
- STFT shape: **(M, N/2 + 1)** complex values

**Numeric Example**:
- Signal: 16,000 samples (1 second at 16 kHz)
- FFT length: N = 512
- Hop size: H = 128
- Number of frames: $M = \lfloor(16000 - 512) / 128\rfloor + 1 = 122$
- Frequency bins: $512/2 + 1 = 257$
- STFT shape: **(122, 257)** complex

### Time–Frequency Trade-offs: The Uncertainty Principle

There's a fundamental trade-off in STFT analysis—you cannot have arbitrarily precise resolution in both time and frequency simultaneously.

**Longer frames** (larger N):
- Better frequency resolution: bins are closer together ($\Delta f = f_s / N$)
- Worse time resolution: each frame spans more time ($\Delta t = N / f_s$)
- Good for: music analysis, pitch detection, stationary signals

**Shorter frames** (smaller N):
- Better time resolution: precise localization of transients
- Worse frequency resolution: bins are farther apart, frequencies blur together
- Good for: onset detection, rapidly changing signals

**The uncertainty principle**: $\Delta t \cdot \Delta f \geq \frac{1}{4\pi}$

**Practical example**:
- N = 2048 at 16 kHz: $\Delta f = 7.8$ Hz, $\Delta t = 128$ ms — great for seeing individual harmonics, poor for timing
- N = 256 at 16 kHz: $\Delta f = 62.5$ Hz, $\Delta t = 16$ ms — better timing, but harmonics blur together

**Common compromise**: N = 512 or 1024 with 50-75% overlap balances both for most speech/audio tasks.

## 2.2 Spectrograms

### From STFT to Spectrogram

The STFT gives complex values. To visualize, we typically discard phase and keep only magnitude or power.

**Magnitude spectrogram**:
$$S_{mag}[m, k] = |X[m, k]|$$

**Power spectrogram**:
$$S_{pow}[m, k] = |X[m, k]|^2$$

**Visualization**: A spectrogram is a 2D image where:
- Horizontal axis: Time (frame index)
- Vertical axis: Frequency (bin index)
- Color/intensity: Magnitude or power

**Text-described diagram**: Imagine a grid where each column is one time frame, each row is one frequency bin. Bright spots indicate strong energy at that time-frequency location. For speech, you'd see horizontal bands (harmonics) that rise and fall as pitch changes, with vertical smears during consonants.

### Log-Spectrogram: Matching Human Perception

Human loudness perception is logarithmic—a sound must increase by ~10× in power to sound "twice as loud."

**Log-power spectrogram** (in decibels):
$$S_{dB}[m, k] = 10 \cdot \log_{10}(S_{pow}[m, k] + \epsilon)$$

where $\epsilon$ is a small constant (e.g., $10^{-10}$) to avoid $\log(0)$.

**Why this matters**:
- Linear spectrograms are dominated by loud components; quiet details are invisible
- Log spectrograms reveal both loud and quiet elements
- Neural networks often learn better from log-spectrograms because the dynamic range is compressed

**Numeric Example**:
- Power values: [0.001, 0.1, 10, 1000]
- Linear range: 1,000,000× (hard to visualize)
- dB values: [-30, -10, 10, 30]
- Log range: 60 dB (much more manageable)

## 2.3 The Mel Scale

### Psychoacoustic Motivation

Human frequency perception is **nonlinear**—we're much better at distinguishing low frequencies than high frequencies.

**Experiment**: Play two tones at 200 Hz and 300 Hz (100 Hz apart). Then play 5000 Hz and 5100 Hz (also 100 Hz apart). The first pair sounds very different; the second pair sounds nearly identical.

The **Mel scale** models this nonlinearity. It's designed so that equal distances on the Mel scale correspond to equal *perceptual* distances.

### Mel Scale Math

The conversion from Hertz to Mel:

$$m = 2595 \cdot \log_{10}\left(1 + \frac{f}{700}\right)$$

The inverse (Mel to Hertz):

$$f = 700 \cdot \left(10^{m/2595} - 1\right)$$

**Numeric examples**:
- 100 Hz → 150 Mel
- 1000 Hz → 1000 Mel (by design, 1000 Hz ≈ 1000 Mel)
- 4000 Hz → 2146 Mel
- 8000 Hz → 2840 Mel

Notice: Going from 100 to 1000 Hz spans 850 Mel. Going from 4000 to 8000 Hz spans only 694 Mel. The Mel scale "compresses" higher frequencies.

### Mel Filterbank

A **Mel filterbank** consists of triangular bandpass filters spaced uniformly on the Mel scale but non-uniformly on the Hz scale.

**Construction**:
1. Choose number of filters (typically 40-128)
2. Define frequency range (e.g., 0 to 8000 Hz)
3. Convert endpoints to Mel scale
4. Space filter center frequencies uniformly in Mel
5. Convert back to Hz → filters are narrow at low frequencies, wide at high frequencies
6. Create triangular filters overlapping at half-height

**Applying the filterbank**: Multiply the power spectrogram by the filterbank matrix:

$$S_{mel}[m, i] = \sum_{k} H[i, k] \cdot S_{pow}[m, k]$$

where $H[i, k]$ is the filterbank matrix (shape: num_mels × num_freq_bins).

**Output**: A **Mel spectrogram** with shape **(M, num_mels)** — fewer frequency channels, but perceptually motivated.

**Why use Mel spectrograms for ML?**
- Dimensionality reduction: 257 freq bins → 80 Mel bands
- Perceptually relevant: focuses resolution where humans care
- Empirically: models trained on Mel spectrograms often outperform those on linear spectrograms

## 2.4 Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs)

### The MFCC Pipeline

MFCCs were the dominant audio feature for speech recognition from the 1980s through ~2012. Understanding them illuminates classical audio feature design.

**Step-by-step pipeline**:

1. **Pre-emphasis**: Apply a first-order high-pass filter to boost high frequencies
   $$y[n] = x[n] - \alpha \cdot x[n-1], \quad \alpha \approx 0.97$$
   Rationale: Speech has more energy at low frequencies; pre-emphasis balances the spectrum.

2. **Framing and windowing**: Chop into 20-25 ms frames with Hamming window.

3. **FFT**: Compute power spectrum for each frame.

4. **Mel filterbank**: Apply triangular Mel filterbank (typically 26-40 filters).

5. **Log compression**: Take the logarithm of filterbank energies.

6. **DCT (Discrete Cosine Transform)**: Apply DCT to decorrelate and compress.
   $$c[i] = \sum_{j=1}^{N_{mel}} \log(S_{mel}[j]) \cdot \cos\left(\frac{\pi i (j - 0.5)}{N_{mel}}\right)$$

7. **Keep first 12-13 coefficients**: Discard higher-order coefficients (which capture fine spectral detail, often noise).

**Output**: For each frame, a vector of 12-13 MFCCs, often augmented with:
- Delta coefficients (first derivative over time)
- Delta-delta coefficients (second derivative)

Total: ~39 features per frame.

### Why MFCCs Worked So Well

1. **Perceptually motivated**: Mel scale matches human hearing.

2. **Decorrelated features**: DCT produces nearly uncorrelated coefficients—ideal for GMMs and diagonal covariance matrices.

3. **Compact representation**: From thousands of samples → 39 numbers per frame.

4. **Robust to channel effects**: Log and DCT make features less sensitive to overall gain.

5. **Matched to HMM-GMM systems**: Gaussian assumptions worked well with MFCC statistics.

### Limitations of Handcrafted Features

**Why modern systems often skip MFCCs**:

1. **Information loss**: Many design choices (number of filters, DCT truncation) discard potentially useful information.

2. **Fixed time resolution**: Frame-level features can't capture sub-frame events.

3. **Not learned**: Neural networks can potentially discover better representations end-to-end.

4. **Phase discarded**: Phase information, useful for source separation and localization, is lost.

**Modern approach**: Feed log-Mel spectrograms (or even raw waveforms) directly to neural networks, letting the network learn optimal features. MFCCs remain useful for lightweight systems and as a baseline.

# 3. Classical Audio & Speech Modeling

Understanding classical methods illuminates why deep learning succeeded and provides valuable intuition about audio structure.

## 3.1 Linear Predictive Coding (LPC)

### Intuition

Speech production can be modeled as an **excitation source** (vocal cords producing buzz or noise) filtered by the **vocal tract** (throat, mouth, tongue shaping the sound). LPC separates these two components.

**The key idea**: The current sample can be predicted as a linear combination of past samples:

$$\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i \cdot x[n-i]$$

where $p$ is the prediction order (typically 10-16 for speech).

### Mathematical Formulation

The **prediction error** (residual) is:
$$e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n] = x[n] - \sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i \cdot x[n-i]$$

LPC finds coefficients $\{a_1, ..., a_p\}$ that minimize the mean squared error:
$$E = \sum_n e[n]^2$$

This is a least squares problem solvable via the **Levinson-Durbin algorithm** in $O(p^2)$ time.

### What LPC Captures

The LPC coefficients encode the **vocal tract transfer function**—the spectral envelope (formant structure). The residual $e[n]$ approximates the excitation signal.

**Applications**:
- Speech compression (CELP codecs in mobile phones)
- Voice conversion (modify LPC coefficients to change speaker characteristics)
- Feature extraction (LPC-derived features complement MFCCs)

## 3.2 Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) for Speech

### Why HMMs for Speech?

Speech is inherently sequential with structure at multiple scales:
- Utterance → Words → Phonemes → Acoustic frames

HMMs model this hierarchy elegantly:
- **Hidden states** represent phonemes or sub-phonetic units
- **Observations** are acoustic features (MFCCs) at each frame
- **Transitions** model temporal dynamics (phoneme durations, sequences)

### HMM Components

An HMM is defined by:
1. **States**: $S = \{s_1, s_2, ..., s_N\}$ — typically 3-5 states per phoneme
2. **Transition probabilities**: $A[i,j] = P(\text{state } j \text{ at } t+1 | \text{state } i \text{ at } t)$
3. **Emission probabilities**: $B[i, o] = P(\text{observation } o | \text{state } i)$ — modeled by GMMs
4. **Initial probabilities**: $\pi[i] = P(\text{start in state } i)$

### The Three HMM Problems

1. **Evaluation**: Given a model and observation sequence, compute $P(\text{observations} | \text{model})$. 
   *Solution*: Forward algorithm — $O(N^2 T)$

2. **Decoding**: Given observations, find the most likely state sequence.
   *Solution*: Viterbi algorithm — dynamic programming, $O(N^2 T)$

3. **Training**: Given observations, learn model parameters.
   *Solution*: Baum-Welch (Expectation-Maximization) algorithm

### HMM-GMM Speech Recognition Pipeline

**Classic ASR architecture** (dominant 1980s-2012):

1. **Feature extraction**: Audio → MFCC frames
2. **Acoustic model**: HMM-GMM scores $P(\text{acoustics} | \text{phoneme})$
3. **Language model**: N-gram scores $P(\text{word sequence})$
4. **Decoding**: Weighted combination via Weighted Finite State Transducers (WFSTs)

**Limitations**:
- GMMs struggle with high-dimensional features
- Shallow architecture limits modeling capacity
- Each component trained separately → suboptimal
- Requires extensive expert-designed phonetic knowledge

---

## 3.3 Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)

### Modeling Emission Probabilities

In HMM-GMM systems, the emission probability for state $i$ is modeled as a GMM:

$$P(o | s_i) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} w_{im} \cdot \mathcal{N}(o; \mu_{im}, \Sigma_{im})$$

where:
- $M$ is the number of mixture components (typically 8-64 per state)
- $w_{im}$ are mixture weights (summing to 1)
- $\mu_{im}$ and $\Sigma_{im}$ are mean and covariance of component $m$

### Why GMMs?

**Advantages**:
- Universal approximators for continuous densities
- Well-understood training (EM algorithm)
- Diagonal covariance assumption works with decorrelated features (MFCCs)

**Limitations**:
- Require many components for complex distributions
- Each frame modeled independently (no context)
- Diagonal covariance ignores feature correlations

**Historical note**: State-of-the-art GMM-HMM systems (e.g., Kaldi) could achieve ~15% WER on Switchboard. Deep learning pushed this below 5%.

## 3.4 Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)

### The Alignment Problem

Different speakers say the same word at different speeds. How do we compare two spoken instances of "hello"?

**DTW** finds the optimal alignment between two sequences that may have different lengths and local timing variations.

### DTW Algorithm

Given two sequences $X = (x_1, ..., x_N)$ and $Y = (y_1, ..., y_M)$:

1. **Compute distance matrix** $D[i,j] = d(x_i, y_j)$ — local distance between all pairs

2. **Find optimal warping path** via dynamic programming:
   $$DTW[i,j] = D[i,j] + \min(DTW[i-1,j], DTW[i,j-1], DTW[i-1,j-1])$$

3. **Final distance**: $DTW[N,M]$ gives the total aligned distance

**Constraints** ensure the path:
- Starts at (1,1) and ends at (N,M)
- Moves monotonically (time doesn't reverse)
- Takes steps of bounded size

### DTW Applications

- **Template-based speech recognition**: Compare unknown utterance to stored templates
- **Speaker verification**: Align and compare speaker utterances
- **Music synchronization**: Align audio to score, or different performances

**Limitations**:
- $O(NM)$ complexity — quadratic in sequence length
- Sensitive to starting/ending silence
- Doesn't generalize well (pure memorization)

## 3.5 Why Classical Pipelines Struggled

### Fundamental Limitations

1. **Shallow modeling**: GMMs and linear models have limited capacity to capture complex acoustic variability.

2. **Modular, not end-to-end**: Each component (features, acoustic model, language model) optimized separately with potentially mismatched objectives.

3. **Hand-engineered features**: MFCCs were designed based on human intuition, not learned from data.

4. **Context limitations**: Frame-level classification ignores long-range dependencies.

5. **Scalability**: Performance plateaued despite more data; couldn't leverage large datasets effectively.

### The Deep Learning Breakthrough

Around 2012, deep neural networks (DNNs) replaced GMMs for emission probabilities, yielding 30% relative error reduction. This opened the floodgates:
- RNNs/LSTMs replaced HMMs for sequence modeling
- End-to-end systems (CTC, attention) eliminated the need for phonetic dictionaries
- Transformers enabled massive scaling

**Key insight**: Given enough data and compute, learned representations outperform hand-crafted features and shallow models.

# 4. Deep Learning for Audio (From First Principles)

## 4.1 Why CNNs Work on Spectrograms

### Spectrograms as Images

A Mel spectrogram has shape **(M, F)** where M is time frames and F is frequency bins. This 2D structure resembles a grayscale image:
- Rows: frequency (like spatial height)
- Columns: time (like spatial width)
- Values: energy (like pixel intensity)

**Crucial observation**: Just as images have local spatial structure (edges, textures), spectrograms have local time-frequency structure (harmonics, formants, onsets).

### Local Patterns in Audio

**Harmonics**: A pitched sound creates horizontal lines (constant frequency over time) at regular vertical intervals (fundamental + overtones).

**Formants**: Resonances of the vocal tract appear as energy concentrations at specific frequencies, shifting smoothly as articulation changes.

**Onsets/Attacks**: Percussive sounds create vertical smears (energy at many frequencies simultaneously, brief in time).

CNNs excel at detecting such local patterns through learned convolutional filters.

### 1D vs. 2D Convolutions for Audio

**1D convolution on raw waveform** (shape: $(1, T)$):
- Filter shape: $(1, K)$ where $K$ is kernel width
- Learns temporal patterns directly from samples
- Examples: WaveNet, Wave-U-Net

**1D convolution on spectrogram** (treating frequency as channels):
- Input shape: $(F, M)$ — F frequency bins, M time steps
- Filter shape: $(F, K)$ — covers all frequencies, K time steps
- Each filter looks at the entire spectrum over a local time window

**2D convolution on spectrogram** (treating as image):
- Input shape: $(1, F, M)$ — single-channel "image"
- Filter shape: $(1, k_f, k_t)$ — local in both time and frequency
- Learns patterns like harmonic stacks, frequency sweeps

**Which to choose?**
- 2D convolution: Standard for classification tasks; exploits time-frequency locality
- 1D convolution: Better when frequency bins aren't spatially meaningful, or for waveform models

---

## 4.2 Receptive Fields in Time and Frequency

### Definition

The **receptive field** of a neuron is the region of the input that affects its activation. For audio CNNs, this determines how much time and frequency context the network can use for each decision.

### Building Large Receptive Fields

**Stacking convolutional layers**: Each layer expands the receptive field.

**Example**: 3×3 kernels, no padding, no pooling:
- Layer 1 receptive field: 3×3
- Layer 2 receptive field: 5×5
- Layer 3 receptive field: 7×7

**Pooling**: Downsamples spatially, rapidly expanding receptive fields but losing resolution.

**Strided convolutions**: Alternative to pooling; convolves with stride > 1.

**Dilated convolutions**: Introduce gaps in the kernel, exponentially expanding receptive fields without pooling (more on this in WaveNet section).

### Practical Receptive Field Design

For a spectrogram with 10 ms hop size:
- Receptive field of 50 frames = 500 ms context
- Speech phonemes are ~80-100 ms; words are 300-600 ms
- You need several hundred ms to capture phonetic context

**Rule of thumb**: For speech classification, ensure your network's receptive field spans at least 500 ms. For speaker recognition, even longer (several seconds) helps.

---

## 4.3 RNNs for Sequential Audio

### Why Sequences Matter

Audio is inherently sequential. The meaning of a sound often depends on what came before and after:
- "I scream" vs. "ice cream" — same acoustics, different segmentation
- Musical context determines whether a note feels resolved or tense

CNNs have fixed receptive fields. RNNs can, in principle, remember arbitrarily far back.

### Vanilla RNN

$$h_t = \tanh(W_{xh} x_t + W_{hh} h_{t-1} + b_h)$$
$$y_t = W_{hy} h_t + b_y$$

**Intuition**: The hidden state $h_t$ is a compressed memory of all past inputs. It's updated at each time step by combining the new input $x_t$ with the previous state $h_{t-1}$.

### The Vanishing Gradient Problem

When training via backpropagation through time (BPTT), gradients must flow through many time steps. With vanilla RNNs, gradients shrink exponentially:

$$\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}} = \prod_{i=t-k}^{t-1} \frac{\partial h_{i+1}}{\partial h_i} \approx (\gamma)^k$$

If $\gamma < 1$, gradients vanish; if $\gamma > 1$, they explode. Either way, long-range dependencies are hard to learn.

### LSTM: Long Short-Term Memory

LSTMs address vanishing gradients with a **gated architecture**:

**Cell state** $c_t$: A "conveyor belt" that can carry information unchanged across time.

**Gates** (all have sigmoid activation, outputting values in [0,1]):
- **Forget gate**: $f_t = \sigma(W_f [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f)$ — what to discard from cell state
- **Input gate**: $i_t = \sigma(W_i [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i)$ — what new information to store
- **Output gate**: $o_t = \sigma(W_o [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o)$ — what to output from cell state

**Update equations**:
$$\tilde{c}_t = \tanh(W_c [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c) \quad \text{(candidate new memory)}$$
$$c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t \quad \text{(update cell state)}$$
$$h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(c_t) \quad \text{(compute output)}$$

**Key insight**: The cell state update $c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + ...$ is additive, not multiplicative. Gradients can flow through unchanged (when $f_t \approx 1$), enabling learning of long-range dependencies.

### GRU: Gated Recurrent Unit

GRUs simplify LSTMs by merging the cell and hidden states:

$$z_t = \sigma(W_z [h_{t-1}, x_t]) \quad \text{(update gate)}$$
$$r_t = \sigma(W_r [h_{t-1}, x_t]) \quad \text{(reset gate)}$$
$$\tilde{h}_t = \tanh(W [r_t \odot h_{t-1}, x_t]) \quad \text{(candidate)}$$
$$h_t = (1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t \quad \text{(interpolate)}$$

**Fewer parameters** than LSTM, often comparable performance. Popular for smaller models.

---

## 4.4 Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs)

### Motivation

RNNs are slow to train (inherently sequential) and can still struggle with very long sequences. TCNs use dilated causal convolutions to capture long-range dependencies efficiently.

### Causal Convolutions

A **causal** convolution only looks at past and present inputs—critical for real-time/streaming applications where future context isn't available.

For a 1D convolution with kernel size $K$:
$$y[t] = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w[k] \cdot x[t-k]$$

No future samples ($t+1, t+2, ...$) are used.

### Dilated Convolutions

**Dilation** inserts gaps in the kernel, expanding the receptive field without adding parameters:

$$y[t] = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w[k] \cdot x[t - d \cdot k]$$

where $d$ is the dilation factor.

**Exponential dilation**: Stack layers with dilation $d = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, ...$

**Numeric example** (kernel size K=2):
- Layer 1 (d=1): looks at $t$ and $t-1$ → receptive field = 2
- Layer 2 (d=2): looks at outputs from $t$ and $t-2$ → RF = 4
- Layer 3 (d=4): looks at outputs from $t$ and $t-4$ → RF = 8
- Layer 4 (d=8): looks at outputs from $t$ and $t-8$ → RF = 16

After 10 layers: receptive field = $2^{10} = 1024$ samples.

**Advantages over RNNs**:
- Fully parallelizable (no sequential dependency)
- Stable gradients (no vanishing/exploding)
- Faster training

---

## 4.5 Loss Functions for Audio

### MSE and L1 for Waveform Prediction

**Mean Squared Error**:
$$\mathcal{L}_{MSE} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} (x[t] - \hat{x}[t])^2$$

**Mean Absolute Error (L1)**:
$$\mathcal{L}_{L1} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} |x[t] - \hat{x}[t]|$$

**Trade-offs**:
- MSE penalizes large errors more → smoother outputs, but vulnerable to outliers
- L1 is more robust → sharper outputs, but can produce discontinuities

**Problem**: Waveform-domain losses don't match perception well. Two waveforms can have high MSE but sound identical (phase shift), or low MSE but sound different (artifacts in perceptually sensitive regions).

### Spectral Losses

**Multi-resolution STFT loss**: Compute STFT at multiple resolutions and sum the losses. This captures both fine and coarse spectral structure.

$$\mathcal{L}_{STFT} = \sum_r \left( \|\log |S_r| - \log |\hat{S}_r|\|_1 + \||S_r| - |\hat{S}_r|\|_{Fro} \right)$$

where $r$ indexes different FFT sizes (e.g., 512, 1024, 2048).

**Mel spectrogram loss**: Compute loss in Mel domain for perceptually weighted errors:

$$\mathcal{L}_{Mel} = \|\text{MelSpec}(x) - \text{MelSpec}(\hat{x})\|_1$$

### Perceptual Losses

For high-quality audio generation, consider:
- **Multi-scale discriminator loss** (adversarial training)
- **Feature matching loss** (match discriminator intermediate representations)
- These are covered in modern vocoders (HiFi-GAN, etc.)

# 5. Canonical Audio Architectures (Evolutionary View)

## 5.1 CNN-Based Audio Classifiers

### Basic Architecture

For audio classification (e.g., speech commands, sound event detection):

**Input**: Log-Mel spectrogram, shape (1, F, M) — 1 channel, F Mel bins, M time frames

**Architecture**:
```
Conv2D(1→32, 3×3) → BatchNorm → ReLU → MaxPool(2×2)
Conv2D(32→64, 3×3) → BatchNorm → ReLU → MaxPool(2×2)
Conv2D(64→128, 3×3) → BatchNorm → ReLU → MaxPool(2×2)
GlobalAveragePool → FC(128→num_classes)
```

**Why this works**:
- Early layers detect local time-frequency patterns (formants, onsets)
- Pooling provides translation invariance (same sound at different times/pitches)
- Later layers combine patterns into higher-level concepts
- Global pooling aggregates across entire spectrogram

### Notable CNN Architectures for Audio

**VGGish** (Google, 2017): VGG-style network trained on YouTube-8M. Often used as a pretrained feature extractor.

**AudioSet models**: Various CNN architectures trained on Google's AudioSet (2M clips, 527 classes).

**PANNs** (Pretrained Audio Neural Networks): ResNet-style CNNs achieving strong performance across audio tasks.

---

## 5.2 CRNNs: CNN + RNN Hybrids

### Motivation

CNNs have fixed receptive fields. RNNs can model variable-length sequences but process spectrograms frame-by-frame without frequency structure. CRNNs combine both strengths.

### Architecture Pattern

```
Input: (batch, 1, F, M)
CNN feature extractor:
    Conv2D layers → output (batch, C, F', M')
Reshape: (batch, M', C*F') — treat time as sequence dimension
RNN (LSTM/GRU):
    BiLSTM(C*F' → H) → output (batch, M', H)
Output layer:
    Either: global pool → FC for classification
    Or: FC at each timestep for sequence labeling
```

**Why the reshape?** After CNN processing, each time step has a feature vector (C×F' channels×frequencies). The RNN then models temporal dependencies across these extracted features.

### Applications

- **Speech emotion recognition**: CNN extracts spectral features, RNN captures temporal dynamics of emotion
- **Music tagging**: CNN detects local musical events, RNN aggregates into song-level tags
- **Sound event detection**: Frame-level predictions with temporal smoothing

---

## 5.3 WaveNet

### Historical Context

WaveNet (DeepMind, 2016) was a breakthrough in audio generation, producing speech quality far surpassing previous methods. It models raw waveforms directly at 16 kHz—predicting 16,000 samples per second.

### Causal Dilated Convolutions

WaveNet uses stacked causal dilated convolutions (as described in Section 4.4) to achieve massive receptive fields while remaining autoregressive.

**Architecture specifics**:
- Dilations: 1, 2, 4, 8, ..., 512 (10 layers), repeated several times
- Receptive field: ~300 ms (sufficient for speech)
- Gated activations: $\tanh(W_f * x) \odot \sigma(W_g * x)$
- Skip connections aggregate outputs from all layers
- Residual connections for gradient flow

### Autoregressive Generation

WaveNet models $P(x_t | x_1, ..., x_{t-1})$—the probability of each sample given all previous samples.

**Training**: Teacher forcing—given ground truth history, predict next sample.

**Generation**: Sample from predicted distribution, append to history, repeat. This is slow (16,000 sequential steps per second of audio) but produces high-quality results.

### Impact and Limitations

**Impact**: Enabled natural-sounding text-to-speech, spawning many follow-up works.

**Limitations**:
- Slow generation (solved by Parallel WaveNet, WaveGlow, HiFi-GAN)
- Requires substantial compute
- Later supplanted by non-autoregressive models for many applications

---

## 5.4 Transformers for Audio

### Self-Attention Equation

The core of transformers is **self-attention**, which allows every position to attend to every other position:

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$$

where:
- $Q = XW_Q$: queries (what am I looking for?)
- $K = XW_K$: keys (what do I contain?)
- $V = XW_V$: values (what information do I provide?)
- $d_k$: key dimension (scaling prevents dot products from growing too large)

**Multi-head attention**: Run attention multiple times with different projections, concatenate results. Allows attending to different aspects simultaneously.

### Transformers for Audio Sequences

**Input**: Typically a sequence of frame-level embeddings from a spectrogram or CNN.

**Positional encoding**: Transformers have no inherent notion of position. Add sinusoidal or learned positional embeddings:
$$PE_{(pos, 2i)} = \sin(pos / 10000^{2i/d})$$
$$PE_{(pos, 2i+1)} = \cos(pos / 10000^{2i/d})$$

**Advantages over RNNs**:
- Direct connections between all positions (no vanishing gradients)
- Parallelizable training (no sequential dependency)
- Excellent at long-range dependencies

**Challenges**:
- Quadratic complexity in sequence length: $O(T^2)$
- Audio sequences can be very long (16 kHz × 10 seconds = 160,000 positions)
- Solutions: Downsampling, local attention, linear attention variants

### Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST)

AST (2021) applies Vision Transformer (ViT) directly to spectrograms:
- Split spectrogram into patches (e.g., 16×16)
- Flatten patches to sequence
- Apply transformer encoder
- Achieves state-of-the-art on AudioSet

---

## 5.5 Conformer Architecture

### Motivation

Transformers excel at global context; CNNs excel at local patterns. Conformer (Google, 2020) combines both for speech recognition.

### Architecture

Each Conformer block contains:
```
Input
  → Feed-Forward Module (½)
  → Multi-Head Self-Attention
  → Convolution Module
  → Feed-Forward Module (½)
  → LayerNorm
Output
```

**Convolution Module**:
- Pointwise conv → GLU → Depthwise conv → BatchNorm → Swish → Pointwise conv
- Captures local patterns efficiently

**Key insight**: Half-step feed-forward layers (inspired by Macaron-style networks) sandwich the attention and convolution, improving gradient flow.

### Impact

Conformer achieved state-of-the-art ASR results on LibriSpeech, demonstrating that hybrid architectures outperform pure transformers for audio.

---

## 5.6 Inductive Biases: CNN vs. RNN vs. Transformer

| Aspect | CNN | RNN | Transformer |
|--------|-----|-----|-------------|
| **Receptive field** | Fixed (by architecture) | Theoretically infinite | Full sequence |
| **Local patterns** | Excellent (by design) | Implicit | Learned |
| **Long-range deps** | Limited (need deep networks) | Can struggle (vanishing gradients) | Excellent |
| **Parallelization** | Excellent | Poor (sequential) | Excellent |
| **Parameter efficiency** | High (weight sharing) | Medium | Lower (attention is dense) |
| **Sequence length** | Efficient | Efficient | Quadratic (standard) |
| **Position encoding** | Implicit (by structure) | Implicit (by sequence) | Must be added |

**Practical guidance**:
- **Short audio, local patterns**: CNN (fast, efficient)
- **Long sequences, streaming**: TCN or RNN
- **Long-range dependencies, abundant data**: Transformer
- **Best of both worlds**: Conformer, CNN+Transformer hybrids

# 6. Task-Specific Audio Models

## 6.1 Audio Classification

### Problem Formulation

Given an audio clip $x$ of duration $D$, predict a class label $y \in \{1, 2, ..., C\}$.

**Examples**:
- Speech commands: "yes", "no", "up", "down", ... (Google Speech Commands dataset)
- Acoustic scene classification: "airport", "street", "park", ... (DCASE challenges)
- Sound event tagging: "dog bark", "car horn", "music", ... (AudioSet)

### Cross-Entropy Loss with Numeric Example

**Softmax activation** converts logits to probabilities:
$$p_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} e^{z_j}}$$

**Cross-entropy loss**:
$$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{i=1}^{C} y_i \log(p_i) = -\log(p_{true})$$

For one-hot labels, this simplifies to the negative log probability of the correct class.

**Numeric example**:
- 3 classes, true label: class 2
- Model outputs logits: $z = [1.0, 2.5, 0.5]$
- Softmax: $p = [e^1, e^{2.5}, e^{0.5}] / (e^1 + e^{2.5} + e^{0.5})$
  - $= [2.72, 12.18, 1.65] / 16.55$
  - $= [0.164, 0.736, 0.100]$
- Loss: $-\log(0.736) = 0.306$

If the model were more confident (logit for class 2 higher), the loss would be lower.

### Metrics

**Accuracy**: Simple but misleading for imbalanced classes.

**Macro F1**: Average F1 across classes (each class weighted equally).

**Micro F1**: Compute F1 from aggregate TP, FP, FN (equivalent to accuracy for single-label).

**mAP (mean Average Precision)**: For multi-label classification, average precision across classes.

---

## 6.2 Speech Recognition (ASR)

### Problem Formulation

Given audio $x$, produce text transcript $y = (y_1, y_2, ..., y_L)$.

**Challenge**: No alignment between audio frames and text characters/words. A 10-second utterance might have 1000 frames and 50 characters—which frames correspond to which characters?

### CTC Loss (Connectionist Temporal Classification)

**Key idea**: Allow the model to output characters at any frame, with a special "blank" token (ε) for silence/pauses. Sum over all alignments that produce the correct transcript.

**CTC decoding rules**:
1. Remove consecutive duplicates
2. Remove blanks

**Example**:
- Model output: `ε-h-h-ε-e-l-l-l-ε-o-ε`
- After removing duplicates: `ε-h-ε-e-l-ε-o-ε`
- After removing blanks: `h-e-l-o` → "helo"

Wait, that's wrong! To output "hello", we need: `h-e-l-ε-l-o` (blank separates the two l's).

**CTC loss**: Sum probability over all valid alignments
$$P(y|x) = \sum_{\pi \in \mathcal{B}^{-1}(y)} P(\pi|x)$$

where $\mathcal{B}^{-1}(y)$ is the set of all alignments mapping to transcript $y$.

**Numeric example (simplified)**:
- Target: "ab"
- Input: 4 frames
- Valid CTC paths: `aab`, `abb`, `a-b`, `-ab`, `a-ab`, etc. (where - is blank)
- CTC computes probability of all these paths efficiently using dynamic programming

### Encoder-Decoder with Attention

**Alternative to CTC**: Sequence-to-sequence models with attention.

**Architecture**:
- **Encoder**: CNN/Transformer processes audio → hidden states $(h_1, ..., h_T)$
- **Decoder**: Autoregressive (Transformer or LSTM) generates text $(y_1, ..., y_L)$
- **Attention**: At each decoder step, attend over encoder states

$$\alpha_i = \text{softmax}(score(s_t, h_i))$$
$$context_t = \sum_i \alpha_i h_i$$

**Attention provides soft alignment**—the model learns which parts of the audio correspond to each output character.

### Word Error Rate (WER)

**Definition**: Minimum edit distance between hypothesis and reference, normalized by reference length.

$$WER = \frac{S + D + I}{N}$$

where:
- S = substitutions
- D = deletions  
- I = insertions
- N = words in reference

**Example**:
- Reference: "the cat sat on the mat"
- Hypothesis: "a cat sat the mat"
- Alignment: `the→a(S) cat=cat sat=sat on→ε(D) the=the mat=mat`
- WER = (1 + 1 + 0) / 6 = 33.3%

**Character Error Rate (CER)**: Same formula but at character level.

## 6.3 Speaker Recognition

### Verification vs. Identification

**Speaker Verification**: Given two audio clips, determine if they're from the same speaker (binary decision). "Is this person who they claim to be?"

**Speaker Identification**: Given an audio clip and a set of known speakers, identify which speaker it is (closed-set) or whether it's an unknown speaker (open-set).

### Embedding Learning (x-vectors)

Modern speaker recognition learns a fixed-dimensional **embedding** (or "x-vector") for each utterance, such that:
- Same-speaker embeddings are close
- Different-speaker embeddings are far apart

**Architecture** (typical):
```
Input: Variable-length spectrogram
Frame-level encoder: TDNN or Transformer
Statistics pooling: Compute mean and std over time → fixed-size vector
Embedding layers: FC → ReLU → FC → embedding (256-512 dim)
```

**Statistics pooling** handles variable length by computing sufficient statistics (mean, standard deviation) over the time dimension.

### Contrastive and Triplet Loss

**Contrastive loss**: For pairs of embeddings $(e_1, e_2)$ with label $y$ (1=same, 0=different):

$$\mathcal{L} = y \cdot d(e_1, e_2)^2 + (1-y) \cdot \max(0, m - d(e_1, e_2))^2$$

where $d$ is distance (cosine or Euclidean) and $m$ is a margin.

**Triplet loss**: For anchor $a$, positive $p$ (same speaker), negative $n$ (different speaker):

$$\mathcal{L} = \max(0, d(a, p) - d(a, n) + m)$$

Push positives closer than negatives by at least margin $m$.

**Modern approaches**: Large-margin softmax variants (AAM-Softmax, ArcFace) work better than triplet loss:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{e^{s \cos(\theta_{y_i} + m)}}{e^{s \cos(\theta_{y_i} + m)} + \sum_{j \neq y_i} e^{s \cos \theta_j}}$$

This adds an angular margin, encouraging larger between-class angles.

## 6.4 Audio Event Detection

### Frame-Level vs. Clip-Level

**Clip-level (tagging)**: Predict which events occur anywhere in the clip (multi-label classification).

**Frame-level (detection)**: Predict which events occur at each frame, outputting temporal boundaries.

### Weak Supervision Challenges

Many audio datasets have only **clip-level labels** (we know a dog barks somewhere in this 10-second clip, but not when).

**Problem**: Training frame-level models requires frame-level labels.

**Solutions**:
- **Multiple Instance Learning (MIL)**: Treat clip as a bag of frames. Clip-level prediction = max or attention-weighted mean over frames. Gradients flow back to frames with high predictions.
- **Attention pooling**: Learn which frames contribute to each class.
- **Semi-supervised methods**: Use clip labels to bootstrap, refine with temporal structure.

### Sound Event Detection Architecture

Typical approach:
```
Input: Log-Mel spectrogram (T frames, F bins)
CNN encoder: Extract frame-level features
RNN/Transformer: Model temporal context  
Output: Per-frame, per-class probabilities (T × C)
```

**Post-processing**: Apply thresholds and minimum duration constraints to produce event segments.

## 6.5 Music Information Retrieval (MIR)

### Beat Tracking

**Problem**: Given audio, find the times of beats (metronome clicks).

**Approach**:
- Extract onset strength envelope (when new events occur)
- Find periodicity (tempo estimation)
- Track beat positions using dynamic programming or neural networks

**Evaluation**: F-measure with tolerance window (typically ±70ms).

### Pitch Estimation

**Problem**: Extract fundamental frequency (f0) over time.

**Challenges**: Polyphonic music, singing vs. instruments, noisy environments.

**Methods**:
- Classical: Autocorrelation, YIN algorithm
- Neural: CREPE (CNN on raw waveform), achieves excellent accuracy

**Output**: Pitch contour in Hz (or MIDI note number) per frame.

### Chord Recognition

**Problem**: Identify chord labels over time (e.g., "Cmaj", "Am", "G7").

**Approach**:
- Extract chroma features (12-dimensional representation collapsing octaves)
- CNN or CRNN classifier over chroma sequences
- HMM or CRF for temporal smoothing (chords don't change every frame)

**Evaluation**: Weighted chord symbol recall (WCSR), chord accuracy with tolerance for similar chords.

# 7. Training & Optimization for Audio Models

## 7.1 Data Augmentation

Audio data augmentation is crucial—models can easily overfit to recording conditions, speakers, or acoustic environments.

### Noise Injection

Add background noise at various SNRs:

$$x_{aug} = x + \alpha \cdot noise$$

where $\alpha$ controls the signal-to-noise ratio.

**Implementation**:
- Collect noise samples (environmental sounds, room tone)
- Mix with clean audio at random SNR (5-20 dB typical)

**Why it works**: Real-world audio is never clean. Training with noise improves robustness.

### Time Stretching

Change speed without changing pitch:

$$x_{aug}[n] = x[n \cdot r]$$

where $r$ is the stretch factor (interpolation needed).

**Effect**: Words/notes are slower or faster but at the same pitch.

**Implementation**: Phase vocoder or WSOLA algorithm; librosa provides `time_stretch()`.

### Pitch Shifting

Change pitch without changing duration:

**Implementation**: Time stretch + resample. Or use phase vocoder.

**Typical range**: ±2-4 semitones (±12-25% frequency shift).

**Why it helps**: Speakers vary in fundamental frequency; instruments may be tuned differently.

### SpecAugment

**SpecAugment** (Google, 2019) applies augmentation directly to spectrograms:

1. **Time warping**: Randomly warp the time axis (slight distortion)
2. **Frequency masking**: Zero out random frequency bands
3. **Time masking**: Zero out random time segments

$$\text{FreqMask}: S[:, f_0:f_0+f] = 0$$
$$\text{TimeMask}: S[t_0:t_0+t, :] = 0$$

**Why it works**:
- Frequency masking forces the model to recognize patterns from partial spectral information
- Time masking simulates missing segments, improving robustness
- Very effective, now standard for ASR

**Numeric example**:
- Spectrogram shape: (100 frames, 80 Mel bins)
- Frequency mask: Zero out 10 consecutive bins (e.g., bins 30-40)
- Time mask: Zero out 15 consecutive frames (e.g., frames 20-35)

## 7.2 Transfer Learning with Pretrained Audio Encoders

### Why Transfer Learning?

Labeled audio data is expensive. Pretrained models capture general audio understanding from large datasets.

### Common Pretrained Models

**VGGish**: CNN trained on YouTube-8M. Extract 128-dim embeddings for downstream tasks.

**YAMNet**: MobileNet trained on AudioSet. Lightweight, good for mobile.

**OpenL3**: Self-supervised embeddings from audio-visual correspondence.

**wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT**: Self-supervised speech models (covered in SOTA section).

### Fine-Tuning Strategies

**Feature extraction**: Freeze pretrained encoder, train only a classifier head.
- Fast, works well with little data
- Limited adaptability

**Full fine-tuning**: Unfreeze all layers, train end-to-end.
- Best performance with enough data
- Risk of catastrophic forgetting

**Layer-wise learning rates**: Higher learning rate for top layers, lower for bottom.
- Balances adaptation and preservation

**Progressive unfreezing**: Start frozen, gradually unfreeze layers during training.
- Reduces instability

---

## 7.3 Handling Variable-Length Audio

Audio clips vary wildly in duration. How do we batch them?

### Padding

Pad shorter sequences to match the longest in the batch:
```python
# Pseudo-code
max_len = max(len(x) for x in batch)
padded = [pad(x, max_len) for x in batch]
```

**Attention mask**: Mark padded positions so the model ignores them.

**Downside**: Wastes computation on padding; very long clips dominate.

### Truncation

Cut all clips to a fixed maximum length:
- For classification, random crop during training (data augmentation)
- For sequence tasks, may lose important content

### Dynamic Batching

Group clips of similar length into the same batch:
- Sort by length
- Batch consecutive samples
- Minimal padding within each batch

Used in ASR systems for efficiency.

### Segment-Level Processing

For very long audio:
- Split into fixed-length segments
- Process independently
- Aggregate predictions (voting, averaging, attention)

---

## 7.4 Class Imbalance

Audio datasets are often severely imbalanced (e.g., 90% of clips are "speech", 0.1% are "gunshot").

### Solutions

**Weighted loss**: Weight each class inversely by frequency:
$$w_c = \frac{N}{C \cdot n_c}$$
where $N$ is total samples, $C$ is number of classes, $n_c$ is samples in class $c$.

**Oversampling minority classes**: Duplicate rare samples in the training set.

**Undersampling majority classes**: Use only a subset of common samples.

**Focal loss**: Down-weight easy examples, focus on hard ones:
$$\mathcal{L}_{focal} = -(1-p_t)^\gamma \log(p_t)$$

where $\gamma$ (typically 2) reduces loss for confident predictions.

---

## 7.5 Overfitting in Low-Resource Regimes

When data is limited (a few hours or less), overfitting is severe.

### Mitigation Strategies

1. **Aggressive augmentation**: SpecAugment, noise, speed perturbation
2. **Strong regularization**: Dropout (0.3-0.5), weight decay
3. **Smaller models**: Fewer parameters = harder to memorize
4. **Transfer learning**: Pretrained initialization
5. **Self-training**: Generate pseudo-labels on unlabeled data
6. **Multi-task learning**: Jointly train on related tasks

**Rule of thumb**: For <10 hours of labeled audio, transfer learning is almost always necessary.

# 8. Evaluation & Metrics

## 8.1 Classification Metrics

### Accuracy

$$\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Correct predictions}}{\text{Total predictions}}$$

**When to use**: Balanced classes, all errors equally bad.

**When to avoid**: Imbalanced classes (predicting majority class always gets high accuracy).

### Precision, Recall, F1

**Precision**: Of positive predictions, how many are correct?
$$P = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}$$

**Recall**: Of actual positives, how many did we find?
$$R = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}$$

**F1-score**: Harmonic mean of precision and recall:
$$F1 = 2 \cdot \frac{P \cdot R}{P + R}$$

### Macro vs. Micro Averaging

**Macro-F1**: Compute F1 for each class, then average.
- Treats all classes equally
- Good when you care about minority classes

**Micro-F1**: Aggregate all TP, FP, FN across classes, then compute F1.
- Dominated by majority classes
- Equals accuracy for single-label classification

### ROC-AUC

**ROC curve**: Plot True Positive Rate vs. False Positive Rate at various thresholds.

**AUC**: Area Under the ROC Curve (1.0 = perfect, 0.5 = random).

**When to use**: Binary classification, evaluating threshold-independent performance.

---

## 8.2 ASR Metrics

### Word Error Rate (WER)

Already covered in Section 6.2. Standard metric for ASR.

**Variants**:
- **CER**: Character-level (useful for morphologically rich languages)
- **Sentence Error Rate (SER)**: Fraction of sentences with any error

### Challenges with WER

- Doesn't weight by importance (getting "not" wrong is worse than getting "the" wrong)
- Sensitive to tokenization and normalization choices
- Doesn't measure understanding (semantic similarity)

---

## 8.3 Signal-Based Metrics

For audio generation and enhancement, we need metrics that measure signal quality.

### Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)

$$SNR = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\sum_n x[n]^2}{\sum_n (x[n] - \hat{x}[n])^2}$$

Higher is better. Simple but doesn't match perception well.

### PESQ (Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality)

**What it does**: Compares degraded speech to reference, outputs a quality score (1-5 scale, like MOS).

**How it works**: Psychoacoustic model simulating human perception, standardized in ITU-T P.862.

**Usage**: `pip install pesq`, requires same sample rate for reference and degraded.

**Limitations**: Only for speech (not music), requires reference signal.

### STOI (Short-Time Objective Intelligibility)

**What it measures**: How intelligible is the speech? (not overall quality)

**Range**: 0 to 1 (higher = more intelligible).

**Correlation**: High correlation with human intelligibility scores.

**Usage**: `pip install pystoi`

---

## 8.4 Subjective vs. Objective Evaluation

### Mean Opinion Score (MOS)

**The gold standard**: Have humans rate audio quality on a 1-5 scale:
- 5: Excellent
- 4: Good
- 3: Fair
- 2: Poor
- 1: Bad

**Expensive**: Requires many listeners, careful experimental design, statistical analysis.

### Objective Metrics as Proxies

PESQ, STOI, and neural MOS predictors (e.g., DNSMOS) estimate MOS without human listening tests.

**Caveat**: No objective metric perfectly predicts human perception. Always validate with real listening tests for important applications.

# 9. State-of-the-Art (SOTA) & Trends

## 9.1 Self-Supervised Audio Learning

### Motivation

Labeled audio is scarce; unlabeled audio is abundant (YouTube, podcasts, recordings). Self-supervised learning (SSL) learns representations from unlabeled data.

### wav2vec 2.0 (Facebook AI, 2020)

**Architecture**:
1. **CNN encoder**: Raw waveform → latent representations $Z$
2. **Quantization module**: Discretize latents via codebook (like VQ-VAE)
3. **Transformer**: Processes quantized representations

**Pretext task**: Mask spans of latent representations, predict the quantized targets.

**Contrastive loss**: Distinguish true quantized target from distractors.

**Results**: With just 10 minutes of labeled data + SSL pretraining, achieves competitive ASR.

### HuBERT (Facebook AI, 2021)

**Key difference from wav2vec 2.0**: Uses offline k-means clustering to generate targets (not learned codebook).

**Iterative refinement**:
1. Extract features from simple model
2. Cluster to get pseudo-labels
3. Train HuBERT to predict these labels
4. Repeat with better features

**Advantage**: Simpler, more stable training than contrastive methods.

### Data2Vec (Facebook AI, 2022)

**Unifying idea**: Self-distillation across modalities (audio, vision, text).

**Approach**: 
- Student network predicts masked positions
- Target = contextualized representation from teacher (EMA of student)
- No quantization, no codebook

**Result**: Competitive across audio, image, and text benchmarks.

### Impact of SSL

Self-supervised pretraining now dominates speech:
- Whisper (OpenAI): Massive multilingual ASR
- XLSR: Cross-lingual speech representations
- AudioLM, MusicLM: Generative audio models

**Key insight**: The representation problem can be largely solved without labels.

---

## 9.2 Foundation Models for Audio

### What is a Foundation Model?

A large model pretrained on broad data, adaptable to many downstream tasks via fine-tuning or prompting.

### Examples

**Whisper** (OpenAI, 2022): 
- 680,000 hours of web audio
- Multilingual ASR and translation
- Robust to noise, accents, domains

**AudioLM** (Google, 2022):
- Generates coherent audio continuations
- Combines semantic and acoustic tokens
- Captures long-range structure

**AudioPaLM** (Google, 2023):
- Speech-to-speech translation
- Preserves speaker voice across languages

### Implications

Foundation models enable:
- Zero-shot transfer to new domains
- Few-shot adaptation with minimal data
- Unified models for multiple audio tasks

**Challenge**: Computational requirements are substantial (training Whisper-large requires thousands of GPU-days).

---

## 9.3 Multimodal Audio-Language Models

### Audio + Text Understanding

**CLAP** (Contrastive Language-Audio Pretraining):
- Aligns audio and text embeddings
- Zero-shot classification: Compare audio embedding to text embeddings of class names
- "A dog barking" → embedding close to actual dog bark audio

**Pengi** (Audio Language Model):
- Generates text descriptions from audio
- Audio captioning, audio QA

### Applications

- **Audio search**: Natural language queries for audio databases
- **Audio captioning**: Describe what's happening in an audio clip
- **Sound event retrieval**: Find sounds matching text descriptions

---

## 9.4 Audio Diffusion Models

### Diffusion for Audio Generation

Diffusion models have revolutionized image generation (DALL-E, Stable Diffusion). Now applied to audio:

**Core idea**: 
- Forward process: Gradually add noise until signal is pure noise
- Reverse process: Learn to denoise, recovering the signal

**AudioLDM** (2023):
- Text-to-audio generation
- Generate sound effects, music, speech from text prompts

**MusicLM** (Google, 2023):
- Text-to-music generation
- High-quality, coherent music from descriptions

**Riffusion**:
- Generate music spectrograms with image diffusion
- Convert spectrograms back to audio

### Vocoders Based on Diffusion

**DiffWave**: Diffusion-based vocoder (spectrogram → waveform).
- Slower than GAN-based vocoders but very high quality

**Grad-TTS**: Diffusion for text-to-speech
- Score-matching approach
- Can trade off quality vs. speed

## 9.5 Real-Time and Low-Latency Audio

### The Latency Challenge

For interactive applications (voice assistants, live translation, gaming), latency matters:
- <100 ms: Feels instantaneous
- 100-300 ms: Noticeable but acceptable
- >300 ms: Disrupts natural conversation

### Sources of Latency

1. **Buffering**: Need a full frame before processing
2. **Model inference**: Computation time
3. **Decoding**: Generating output (especially autoregressive)
4. **Network**: For cloud-based systems

### Techniques for Low Latency

**Streaming models**: Process audio incrementally, don't wait for full utterance.
- Streaming ASR: Emit partial transcripts
- Causal models: Only use past context

**Efficient architectures**: 
- Depthwise separable convolutions
- Linear attention variants
- Knowledge distillation to smaller models

**Hardware acceleration**: TensorRT, ONNX Runtime, specialized ASICs.

---

## 9.6 Edge Deployment for Audio Systems

### Why Edge?

- **Privacy**: Audio doesn't leave device
- **Latency**: No network round-trip
- **Reliability**: Works offline
- **Cost**: No cloud compute

### Challenges

- Limited memory (MBs, not GBs)
- Limited compute (CPU, maybe small NPU)
- Battery constraints

### Techniques

**Quantization**: 
- Float32 → Int8 (4× smaller, faster on integer units)
- Usually <1% accuracy loss with careful calibration

**Pruning**:
- Remove unimportant weights (set to zero)
- Structured pruning removes entire channels/layers

**Knowledge distillation**:
- Train small "student" to mimic large "teacher"
- Student captures essential behavior in fewer parameters

**Architecture design**:
- MobileNets, EfficientNets designed for edge
- Streaming architectures for real-time

**Example**: Google's on-device speech recognition achieves real-time ASR with <100 MB model.

# 10. Real-World Audio System Design

## 10.1 End-to-End vs. Modular Pipelines

### Modular Pipeline

Classic approach with specialized components:
```
Audio → Feature Extraction → Acoustic Model → Language Model → Decoder → Text
```

**Advantages**:
- Each component interpretable and debuggable
- Can improve components independently
- Incorporate expert knowledge (lexicons, grammars)

**Disadvantages**:
- Each component optimized separately (mismatched objectives)
- Error propagation between components
- Complex engineering

### End-to-End Systems

Single neural network: Audio → Text

**Advantages**:
- Jointly optimized for final objective
- Simpler pipeline
- Can learn features beyond human intuition

**Disadvantages**:
- Requires massive data
- Less interpretable
- Harder to incorporate external knowledge

### Hybrid Approaches

Modern systems often blend both:
- Neural acoustic model + neural language model + beam search
- Pretrained encoder + task-specific head
- Foundation model + retrieval augmentation

---

## 10.2 Streaming vs. Offline Inference

### Offline Processing

Process entire audio file at once:
- Full context available (past and future)
- Batch processing for efficiency
- Higher accuracy possible

**Use cases**: Transcription of recordings, content moderation, media analysis.

### Streaming Processing

Process audio incrementally as it arrives:
- Causal: Only past context available
- Frame-by-frame or chunk-by-chunk
- Must output partial results

**Use cases**: Voice assistants, live captioning, real-time translation.

### Streaming Architecture Considerations

**Look-ahead**: Small future context (100-200 ms) dramatically improves accuracy with minor latency hit.

**Endpointing**: Detect when user has stopped speaking to trigger response.

**Partial/final hypothesis**: Emit unstable partials, then finalize once confident.

**State management**: Maintain context across chunks.

---

## 10.3 Latency Constraints

### Latency Budget Breakdown

For a voice assistant responding in <500 ms total:
- Audio capture/buffering: 50 ms
- Wake word detection: 50 ms
- ASR: 200 ms
- NLU + Logic: 50 ms
- TTS generation: 100 ms
- Audio playback: 50 ms

**Implication**: Each component has tight constraints. ASR getting 200 ms total might process audio in chunks of 20 ms.

### Strategies

**Speculative execution**: Start NLU while ASR is still running.

**Streaming TTS**: Begin speaking response before full generation.

**Caching**: Precompute common responses.

**Tiered processing**: Fast local model for common cases, cloud for complex ones.

---

## 10.4 Robustness to Noise and Domain Shift

### Noise Robustness

**Training time**: Augment with noise, reverberation, room simulation.

**Inference time**: Front-end enhancement (denoising, dereverberation).

**Architecture**: Multi-condition training, noise-aware models.

### Domain Shift

Model trained on one domain (broadcast news) often fails on another (call center).

**Causes**:
- Different vocabulary
- Different acoustic conditions
- Different speaking styles

**Solutions**:
- Domain adaptation (fine-tuning on target domain)
- Multi-domain training
- Domain-adversarial training
- Test-time adaptation

---

## 10.5 Dataset Bias

### Common Biases

**Accent/dialect**: Models trained on American English struggle with Indian English.

**Gender**: May perform worse on underrepresented genders.

**Age**: Child speech, elderly speech differ from adult.

**Language**: Most resources for high-resource languages (English, Mandarin).

**Environment**: Clean studio recordings vs. noisy real-world.

### Mitigation

**Data collection**: Actively sample underrepresented groups.

**Data augmentation**: Simulate diverse conditions.

**Evaluation**: Report performance broken down by demographics.

**Fairness constraints**: Equalize error rates across groups.

---

## 10.6 Monitoring Audio Models in Production

### What to Monitor

**Input quality**:
- Audio SNR, clipping, silence ratio
- Sampling rate mismatches

**Model behavior**:
- Confidence distributions
- Latency per request
- Memory/compute utilization

**Output quality**:
- User corrections/rejections
- Downstream task performance
- A/B test metrics

### Detecting Degradation

**Data drift**: Input distribution changes (new acoustic environments, speakers).

**Concept drift**: Relationship between input and output changes (new vocabulary).

**Detection methods**:
- Statistical tests on feature distributions
- Anomaly detection on confidence scores
- Human-in-the-loop sampling for quality review

### Continuous Improvement

**Feedback loops**: Collect corrections to create new training data.

**Active learning**: Prioritize labeling uncertain samples.

**Regular retraining**: Scheduled model updates with new data.
